01 — The Story

A Boy Between Two Countries

Paperboy is a genre-breaking literary debut — a novel with a memoir's pulse: the immigrant story, retold at kid height, on a paper route, in the fog.

Mama called it an upgrade.

March 1981. Nine-year-old Jojo boards a plane in Manila — leaving behind the maids, the driver, and a father the newspapers call a statesman — and lands in San Francisco, where the fog never lifts and the upgrade turns out to be a walk-up flat, a quiet stranger called Uncle Rey, and a canvas bag of newspapers heavier than he is.

Over three years, Jojo learns America the hard way: block by block, curb by curb, quarter by quarter. Little League and liquor-store arcades. Heavy metal and Catholic guilt. Neighbors with new names for him, none of them his. A lawsuit that empties the family coffee can. And a best friend on a Mongoose who teaches him the one thing the letters home never mention — how to stay upright when the hill drops out from under you.

Begun as a high-school English assignment in 1986 and forged over forty years, Paperboy is funny and brutal in the same breath: a story about class, race, and belonging; about the friendships that shape us and the ones that break something we didn't know could break; about a boy caught between two countries, neither of which entirely wants to claim him.

This is a novel about growing up between the country you lost and the one you're still trying to earn — and about finding, in the most unlikely places, someone glad you came.

Read the Opening — Chapter One +

Fresh Off the Sushi Boat

Mama called it an upgrade. She said the word the way she said grace — fast, certain, and only once.

She'd written it in the letters, the ones that came in thin blue airmail envelopes with American stamps and her convent handwriting, every loop identical, like she'd practiced being legible her whole life because someone had once told her illegible girls didn't get hired. Upgrade. Jojo turned the word over the way he turned over the coins Kuya Bert let him keep from the ashtray in the car. It sounded like a toy you got for behaving. It sounded like something with a bag and a receipt.

He was nine. His birthday was September 30, 1971, which on March 3, 1981, made him nine exactly — no fractions, no almost. Still short enough that the airplane seatbelt cut across his stomach instead of his hips. Still the kind of nine-year-old who believed the things his mother put in writing, because his mother did not write things that weren't true, she only left things out, which — he would spend the rest of his life discovering — is a different skill entirely, and a more useful one.

What he was leaving: a house with three maids; a cook, a cleaner, and a nanny. A driver named Kuya Bert — "kuya" is a title reserved for all the men who did not yet earn the respects of a Tito — who let him sit up front and hold the wheel at forty kilometers an hour, both of them laughing like it was a heist…

03 — The Author

Joselito "Jojo" Sering

JS

Joselito "Jojo" Sering was born in Manila and landed in San Francisco in 1981, nine years old. Paperboy began as a memoir written for an English class at El Camino High School in 1986 and took forty years to finish. In between, he fronted a metal band, released electronic records, taught filmmaking, and wrote, directed, and scored the award-winning animated feature Love Letter Templates (2021). In 2023, after reconnecting with his mother's side of the family and visiting her home village for the first time, he pursued and regained his Philippine dual citizenship under the birthright provisions of Republic Act 9225. He is an audiovisual engineer with IATSE Local 16 and recently launched AIMA, an AI media agency — the venture that made this literary debut possible. He lives in San Francisco with his wife of thirty years and their son.
