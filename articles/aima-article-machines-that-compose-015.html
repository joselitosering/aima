MACHINES THAT COMPOSE: WHAT AI MUSIC REVEALS ABOUT HUMAN CREATIVITY

JS
Joselito Sering
Editor-in-Chief · AIMA
Published
June 20, 2026
Read Time
9 min read
Category
AI Creative

Last year, a grieving daughter uploaded her late mother's voicemails to an AI audio platform and heard her mother sing a lullaby — in a voice she never actually used. The clip spread quietly through grief support communities before vanishing without comment. Nobody knew whether to call it beautiful or disturbing. We still don't. That uncertainty, sitting at the precise intersection of technology and what we consider deeply human, is exactly what AI music forces us to confront.

THE MACHINES CAN ALREADY PLAY

Suno. Udio. AIVA. MusicGen. The names sound like characters from a dystopian novel, but they are already operating inside the music industry — and in some cases, outperforming it on pure throughput.

Text-to-music platforms like Suno, which crossed 12 million users by early 2025, can generate a complete, radio-ready song — lyrics, vocals, instrumentation, in any genre — in under 30 seconds from a text prompt. Udio, its closest rival, produces audio with a fidelity that has fooled trained musicians in blind listening tests. Meta's open-source MusicGen puts the same capability in any developer's hands. AIVA has been quietly scoring film trailers and video game environments for European studios since 2016. The barrier between text prompt and polished audio is essentially gone.

Ask Suno for a melancholy jazz ballad about the San Francisco fog and you will get something that could credibly appear on a Spotify playlist. Ask Udio for 1980s synth-pop with a driving kick drum and you'll receive something that belongs on a John Hughes soundtrack. The output is technically stunning. Which is precisely why what it reveals about human creativity is so worth examining.

The Recording Industry Association of America is not pleased. In June 2024, it filed copyright infringement suits against both Suno and Udio, alleging the companies trained their models on copyrighted recordings without permission or compensation. The suits remain among the most consequential intellectual property battles in technology history — not because of the dollar amounts, but because of what they'll decide: whether AI music systems can legally consume recorded human artistry as raw material for generating commercial products.

That case is still playing out. But the machines aren't waiting for the verdict.

THE OLDEST DEBATE IN ART GETS A SYNTHESIZER

The panic about AI replacing human creativity is not new. It just has better speakers now.

When composer David Cope's program EMI (Experiments in Musical Intelligence) began generating music in the style of Bach, Chopin, and Beethoven in the 1980s, it provoked genuine professional fury. Philosopher Hubert Dreyfus argued the outputs were hollow — technically correct but spiritually empty. Others pointed out that EMI's Chopin études were indistinguishable from the real thing in blind auditions. What that proved, exactly, remained contested for decades. Cope eventually abandoned the project under sustained pressure, though he left behind thousands of AI-generated compositions that continue to circulate.

Before Cope, John Cage spent decades arguing that all sound could be music — that creativity was fundamentally a matter of intention and framing, not technical production. His 1952 piece 4'33" requires performers to sit silently onstage while the audience listens to ambient noise. Is a Suno track less "composed" than that? The question isn't rhetorical. It's the oldest question in aesthetics wearing new clothes.

What AI music actually reveals is that we have never had a clean, agreed-upon definition of musical creativity. We know it when we hear it — or believe we do — but every time someone has tried to formalize that knowledge, the boundary has shifted. Electronic instruments were denounced as inauthentic. Sampling was called theft. Auto-Tune was a moral failing. Each time, the industry absorbed the technology, assigned it cultural meaning, and called the result art. The argument against AI music today is structurally identical to the argument against the electric guitar in 1955.

🎼
The Long History of "This Isn't Real Music"
The piano was considered a mechanical interloper in 18th-century concert halls. The phonograph was denounced as a destroyer of live performance culture. Synthesizers were called anti-human. Drum machines were said to steal session work. Every generation has a machine it believes crosses the line. None of the lines held.

THE AUTHORSHIP TRAP

In November 2023, The Beatles released "Now and Then" — a song reconstructed from a 1970s John Lennon demo cassette, using AI to isolate Lennon's voice from background noise that had made the recording unusable for fifty years. It debuted at number one in the UK. Paul McCartney called it "the last Beatles record." It was emotional, meticulously crafted, and made with the explicit intention of honoring a lost collaborator.

That same month, an anonymous producer uploaded a track built from AI-generated vocal imitations of Drake and The Weeknd — neither of whom had any involvement whatsoever. It streamed millions of times before Universal Music Group forced its removal from platforms. The synthetic voices were indistinguishable from the real artists to nearly every listener who heard it.

Two songs. One month. Completely different ethical situations — and the generative model technology that made them possible was essentially the same.

This is the authorship trap: AI music doesn't just blur the line between human and machine. It blurs the line between tribute and theft, between tool and replacement, between authentic voice and synthetic imitation. The law hasn't caught up because the ethics haven't either. We don't yet have a shared framework for deciding which uses of AI-generated sound are meaningful and which are exploitative — and "I know it when I hear it" doesn't survive contact with the technology.

Some artists have navigated this by claiming the technology rather than being claimed by it. Grimes explicitly opened her voice model to collaborators in April 2023, offering 50% royalties on any AI-generated track using her vocal likeness. Holly Herndon built "Holly+" — an AI instrument trained on her own voice that any fan can play, turning her likeness into a collaborative instrument rather than a thing that can be stolen. Both approaches preserve one essential element: intentionality. The human still decides what the technology is for.

⚠️
The RIAA Lawsuits — What's Actually at Stake
The RIAA's 2024 infringement suits against Suno and Udio allege that training AI music models on copyrighted recordings constitutes mass unlicensed copying — not transformative use. If upheld, the ruling would force AI music platforms to license training data retroactively or rebuild their models entirely. The outcome will set the economic and legal foundation for the entire generative audio industry.

WHAT THE ALGORITHM CANNOT HEAR

Ask any working musician what makes a performance matter and you will not get a description of technical execution. You will get a story: the session where grief crept into a take that wasn't supposed to be emotional, the live performance where something broke and the band played through it anyway, the lyric that meant something different the second time you heard it because your life had changed. Meaning in music is not encoded in the audio file. It arrives through the relationship between a listener's life and an artist's intentional choices.

A generative model does not have grief. It has statistical patterns extracted from the outputs of people who did.

This distinction matters more than it first appears. When Billie Eilish whispers a lyric, the restraint is intentional — shaped by specific emotional choices, specific life experiences, a specific decision about vulnerability and withholding. An AI trained on her vocal style can approximate the technique. It cannot reproduce the intention. It doesn't know why the line was whispered. It doesn't know what the whisper cost.

The philosopher Susanne Langer argued that music is "the tonal analogue of emotive life" — it doesn't express emotion directly, it embodies the form of feeling. That distinction is precise and useful here. A melody can take the shape of longing without the model that generated it having longed for anything. The shape is learnable. The lived referent is not. This is not a mystical claim about human souls. It is a functional observation about what information is and is not present in a training corpus.

A machine can learn the shape of longing. It cannot know what it cost to have longed.

— AIMA Editorial

This is not an argument that AI music is worthless. It is an argument about what it is, and for what purposes it is suited. If you need 90 seconds of emotionally appropriate underscore for a product video, AI music can do that work faster and cheaper than any human composer. If you need an album that means something — that carries the weight of a person's inner life into your car at 11 PM on a drive you weren't expecting to feel anything on — you are still looking for a human who has lived enough to have something to say.

THE COMPOSER IS NOT REPLACED — THEY'RE REASSIGNED

Here is what the panic about AI music consistently misunderstands: the most valuable creative function has always been judgment, not production.

Brian Eno didn't revolutionize ambient music because he played synthesizers faster than anyone else. He did it because he had a coherent vision of what music could be — formless, environmental, anti-narrative — and he kept making decisions that moved toward that vision. His tools changed constantly. Reel-to-reel tape gave way to modular synthesis gave way to generative systems. The tools were never the point. The accumulated aesthetic intelligence guiding their use was irreplaceable.

The same logic applies now. Suno and Udio give anyone the ability to generate a technically competent track in any genre on demand. What they don't provide is the taste to know which track to make, the cultural awareness to understand what it should mean, or the artistic integrity to know when to discard everything and start again from silence. The latent diffusion model has no aesthetic commitments. That's exactly why it needs a human director.

The artists who are flourishing in this environment treat AI as a compositional sketch pad — a way to prototype emotional territories before committing human craft to a final form. Independent producers are using AI-generated loops as beds for live vocal arrangements. Film composers are exploring mood variations across dozens of generated options before deciding on instrumentation and orchestration. The floor of musical production has been lowered to near zero. The ceiling for what a committed human artist can achieve with these tools has not moved — and in some cases has risen.

What has changed is the middle. The competent-but-uninspired session work, the library music, the genre-fill that existed because production was expensive and human time was the cheapest variable. That market is genuinely disrupted. For the people who built livelihoods there, this is serious and worth acknowledging plainly. But the disruption also reflects something that was always structurally true: that work was primarily about technical execution, and technical execution is exactly what machines are designed to optimize.

✓
What Artists Are Actually Doing With AI Music Tools
Rather than treating AI as a replacement, working artists are using generative tools as accelerators: AI-generated instrumental beds under live vocals, rapid prototype sketches for melody and arrangement, mood exploration across dozens of variations before committing to a direction. The human role shifts from execution to curation and direction — arguably the more creative function.

WHAT WE ACTUALLY OWE MUSIC

There is a deeper question underneath all of this, and it doesn't have a comfortable answer.

If a generative model can produce music that causes the same neurological responses in a listener as human-made music — the same dopamine release, the same emotional resonance, the same sense of recognition — does the origin matter? Is authenticity about the listener's experience, or about the creator's intention? Is it about the sound, or about the story of how the sound came to exist?

We've never had to answer this question seriously before because the origin was always either obvious or irrelevant. Now it is neither. A playlist served by a streaming algorithm may already contain tracks where you genuinely cannot tell, and increasingly won't be able to tell, whether a human being spent their creative life arriving at the choices you are hearing or whether a model arrived at statistically similar choices in thirty seconds. How we respond to that fact will reveal what we actually believe music is for.

AIMA's position is not that AI music is bad. It's that the conversation about it is almost universally unserious. The people celebrating it most loudly often treat production quality as the only metric worth measuring. The people condemning it most fiercely often retreat to vague mysticism about irreducible human souls without engaging with what's actually technically happening. Neither camp is helping anyone think clearly about what is at stake.

What AI music reveals about human creativity, finally, is this: creativity was never really about the ability to produce sound. It was about having something to say — something shaped by a specific life, a specific set of losses, a specific vision of what the world could be — and finding a form in which to say it. The machines have learned to speak. The humans still have to decide what is worth saying. That is not a small thing. It is, in fact, the only thing that ever mattered.

Glossary

Text-to-Music ↑ Back to text

A class of AI system that generates original audio — including melody, harmony, instrumentation, and vocals — from a written text prompt. Unlike earlier algorithmic composition tools, modern text-to-music models can produce complete songs in multiple genres within seconds. Leading examples include Suno, Udio, Meta's MusicGen, and Google's MusicLM. The underlying technology typically uses transformer architectures or diffusion models trained on large datasets of recorded music and text descriptions.

Generative Model ↑ Back to text

A machine learning system trained to generate new data — images, text, audio, video — that statistically resembles its training set. Generative models do not "understand" what they produce in any cognitive sense; they identify statistical patterns in training data and produce novel outputs consistent with those patterns. In the context of AI music, a generative model learns the structural and acoustic properties of recorded songs and generates new audio that follows similar patterns without having any intention, emotion, or lived experience behind the output.

EMI (Experiments in Musical Intelligence) ↑ Back to text

A pioneering algorithmic composition program developed by composer and computer scientist David Cope beginning in the 1980s. EMI analyzed existing compositions to extract stylistic rules and then generated new works in the style of classical composers including Bach, Chopin, and Mozart. Its outputs were sufficiently convincing that trained musicians sometimes could not distinguish them from authentic works in blind tests. EMI generated widespread debate about whether algorithmic composition constitutes creativity and was eventually shut down by Cope after sustained criticism from the musical community.

Latent Diffusion ↑ Back to text

A class of generative AI model that learns to create data by reversing a "noise addition" process. During training, the model learns to progressively denoise randomly corrupted samples. During generation, it starts from pure noise and iteratively refines it into a coherent output guided by a text prompt or other conditioning signal. Latent diffusion models power many leading AI image generators (Stable Diffusion) and are increasingly used in audio generation. They produce high-quality, diverse outputs but operate entirely through statistical pattern matching — not through understanding or intention.

Library Music ↑ Back to text

Pre-composed music created specifically for use in commercial productions — advertisements, television, film, video games, and digital media — rather than for concert performance or commercial release as standalone recordings. Library music is typically sold or licensed on a per-use or subscription basis and covers a wide range of genres and moods. Because library music prioritizes functional fit over artistic expression, it represents the segment of the music industry most immediately disrupted by AI generation tools, which can produce genre-appropriate, technically competent tracks on demand.

Fair Use (Training Data)

A legal doctrine under U.S. copyright law that permits limited use of copyrighted material without permission under certain conditions — traditionally for purposes such as commentary, criticism, education, or parody. Whether training an AI model on copyrighted music constitutes fair use is the central legal question in the RIAA's 2024 suits against Suno and Udio. AI companies argue that training is transformative and produces new works; rights holders argue it constitutes mass copying for commercial gain. Courts have not yet issued a definitive ruling, making this one of the most consequential unresolved questions in contemporary IP law.
