DIAGNOSIS BY ALGORITHM: THE STATE OF AI IN CLINICAL MEDICINE, 2026

JS
Joselito Sering
Editor-in-Chief · AIMA
Published
June 28, 2026
Read Time
13 min read
Category
AI Healthcare

Somewhere in a dermatologist's office, an AI flags a lesion on pale skin with near-perfect precision. In another clinic, the same algorithm examines darker skin and is effectively guessing almost half the time. That disparity isn't a bug anyone filed a report on — it's a policy consequence, encoded in training data and now deployed at scale. This is what it means to practice medicine in 2026: surrounded by tools that are genuinely remarkable and, in specific and measurable ways, genuinely dangerous to the patients who need them most.

The scale of AI in clinical medicine is no longer theoretical. According to the American Medical Association's 2026 Physician Survey on Augmented Intelligence, 81% of U.S. physicians now use AI in their practices — more than double the 38% who reported doing so in 2023. The FDA has cleared more than 1,400 AI-enabled medical devices, 295 of them in 2025 alone, from 221 manufacturers. The tools are here. The question is which ones work, for whom they work, and who absorbs the cost when they don't.

The honest accounting, as of mid-2026, is that clinical AI is simultaneously the most evidence-backed application of machine learning in the real world and the most consequential place to deploy systems that haven't been tested across the full range of patients who will eventually use them. The same technology shortening the distance between molecular discovery and clinical cure in oncology is producing diagnostic blind spots that follow fault lines already carved by race, age, and geography. Understanding this field requires holding both truths without flinching from either.

1,400 Cleared, and Counting

The FDA's authorized AI-enabled device list crossed 1,400 entries in mid-2026. Radiology accounts for roughly 75% of those clearances — an artifact of digital imaging's head start and the relative legibility of image classification as a task. The remaining quarter spans pathology, cardiology, ophthalmology, dermatology, and clinical decision support. The median clearance time is 142 days. Twenty-four percent of 2025 clearances were processed in under 90 days.

Those numbers sound like momentum, and in some ways they are. What they obscure is the pathway. Ninety-six percent of AI device clearances came through the 510(k) route — a mechanism designed for devices that are "substantially equivalent" to something already on the market. It was built for incremental hardware updates, and it has become the default for a category of tools that can behave in fundamentally novel ways. Think of it like certifying a self-driving vehicle because it has four wheels and a steering column in roughly the same places as a car that already exists. The frame is familiar. The behavior is not.

Less than 10% of 2025 AI clearances included a Predetermined Change Control Plan — the FDA's mechanism that allows manufacturers to update algorithms without new submissions. That means most cleared AI tools are regulatory snapshots, frozen at clearance while the underlying technology and the clinical landscape both continue to move. In January 2026, the FDA issued guidance that further loosened oversight for low-risk clinical decision support software — expanding enforcement discretion, permitting single AI recommendations rather than option sets, and declining to define what "clinically appropriate" means. That definition was left to developers. The guidance also said nothing about large language models or probabilistic outputs, which now constitute a growing share of what physicians actually encounter.

The Reimbursement Desert. FDA clearance does not equal clinical deployment. Of more than 1,400 cleared AI tools, only two CPT Category I codes exist for AI-specific clinical functions — and both are in cardiac imaging. A tool can win regulatory approval in 142 days and then sit unused in a hospital system because no payer will reimburse it. That gap between what regulators approve and what economics allow is the single largest structural barrier to broad clinical adoption in 2026.

Where It Actually Works

The strongest evidence for clinical AI lives in radiology, and the strongest evidence within radiology lives in tasks that are narrow, well-defined, and visually discrete. Stroke triage is the clearest success story: AI for large vessel occlusion detection and intracranial hemorrhage identification has moved from research to routine deployment in major stroke centers, cutting time-to-diagnosis in emergent cases by up to 90% in some systems. That number translates directly into fewer dead neurons, fewer disabled patients, and outcomes that are materially better than what the same physicians achieve without AI assistance. It is exactly the kind of result that justifies building this technology.

Lung nodule detection sits in the same category of genuine, reproducible evidence. A systematic review of 150 peer-reviewed AI clinical medicine studies found that the best systems achieve area-under-the-curve scores up to 0.94 for lung nodule detection — performance that meets or exceeds the sensitivity of experienced radiologists working without AI assistance. In mammography, AI reduced false-positive marks by 69% compared to traditional computer-aided detection systems. In digital pathology, AI assistance raised pathologist sensitivity for lymph node metastasis detection from 83% to 91%. These are not projected improvements. They are measured outcomes in clinical practice.

A Routine ECG, Transformed. Cardiac amyloidosis is a disease that usually announces itself too late — its symptoms are nonspecific enough that diagnosis typically comes at an advanced stage. Anumana's FDA-cleared ECG-AI algorithm changes that math: validated across more than 15,000 patients, it detects cardiac amyloidosis from a standard 12-lead ECG with 78.9% sensitivity and 91.2% specificity. Mayo Clinic cardiologist Martha Grogan framed the clinical significance directly: "A tool that helps clinicians recognize suspicion of amyloidosis from a routine ECG could support earlier diagnosis and more timely next steps in care." What was once a disease requiring expensive, specialized imaging to even suspect can now be flagged by a test available in any clinic with an ECG machine.

Sepsis prediction occupies more complicated territory. AI systems deployed in intensive care units and emergency departments can flag sepsis onset three to twelve hours before a physician places the first antibiotic order — a window that, when acted upon correctly, saves lives. The COMPOSER system has demonstrated early warning 12.2 hours before antibiotics in the ICU and 2.1 hours in the emergency department. The evidence base is real. The challenge is what happens when the alert fires. Alarm fatigue — the desensitization that sets in when a system produces too many false positives — has emerged as a documented failure mode that can wipe out the clinical benefit of a statistically strong tool. The algorithm performs; the culture around the algorithm does not always follow.

And then there is drug discovery, where AI has produced what may be its most dramatic proof of concept. Daraxonrasib — a RAS inhibitor identified through AI-driven network biology analysis — was presented at the American Society of Clinical Oncology's 2026 annual meeting to a standing ovation. In a Phase 3 trial for previously treated metastatic pancreatic cancer, it produced median overall survival of 13.2 months versus 6.7 months for chemotherapy, with a hazard ratio of 0.40. Darwin Health co-founders Andrea Califano and Gideon Bosker argued in STAT News that AI network biology was essential to identifying the RAS-targeted approach — that no other method could have navigated the molecular complexity of a cancer that had resisted every prior strategy. The FDA granted it Breakthrough Therapy and Orphan Drug designation. The drug was not found without AI. That is a sentence worth sitting with.

AI doesn't replace the radiologist. It changes what the radiologist catches, and how fast. That's a different kind of transformation — quieter than replacement, and more durable.

— Joselito Sering, AIMA

The Reasoning Gap

In April 2026, a research team at Mass General Brigham and Harvard Medical School published a study in JAMA Network Open that tested 21 large language models — including ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok, and DeepSeek — on 29 clinical cases. The headline result: more than 80% of the time, these models failed to generate clinically adequate differential diagnoses. This despite achieving final diagnosis accuracy exceeding 90% when given complete patient data.

The distinction between those two numbers is the entire story. Final diagnosis accuracy measures whether a model can name the correct answer once the puzzle is already assembled. Differential diagnosis is the actual work of medicine — generating a ranked list of possible conditions to investigate when a patient arrives with symptoms but no answers, then holding that uncertainty open while evidence accumulates. It is the cognitive work that happens before the right answer is known, under time pressure, with incomplete information and a real person waiting. Lead author Arya Rao put it plainly: "Models excel at naming a final diagnosis once the data is complete, but they struggle at the open-ended start of a case." Co-author Marc Succi, Executive Director of the MESH Incubator at Mass General Brigham, named the gap more precisely: "Differential diagnoses are central to clinical reasoning and underlie the 'art of medicine' that AI cannot currently replicate."

When the Old System Wins. In a separate head-to-head comparison at Massachusetts General Hospital, researchers tested DXplain — a rule-based expert diagnostic system built in the 1980s — against ChatGPT-4 and Gemini 1.5 Pro across 36 general medicine cases. With complete lab data, DXplain achieved 72% accuracy. ChatGPT reached 64%. Gemini 58%. Without lab data: DXplain 56%, ChatGPT 42%, Gemini 39%. The differences were not statistically significant, but the direction was consistent across all conditions. The most hyped AI tools in the world lost to a decades-old system purpose-built for the task. The MGH authors suggested the answer may be a hybrid — combining LLMs' natural language processing with deterministic expert-system logic — but that hybrid does not yet exist in deployed clinical form.

Into this context, in May 2026, Google published a study in Nature Medicine showing that its AMIE multimodal AI outperformed primary care physicians on 29 of 32 evaluation axes across 105 simulated clinical cases, including 7 of 9 multimodal reasoning metrics. Patient actors also rated AMIE as more empathetic and trustworthy than human physicians. The study is the most rigorous AI-versus-physician diagnostic comparison published to date. It is also a simulation, using scripted patient actors in a remote chat interface, not real patients presenting to a real clinic. Google's own authors note that "further research is needed before real-world translation." A prospective trial is underway at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. No outcome data from that trial has been published.

STAT News asked the question directly in a May 2026 piece: "Did AI Really Beat Doctors at Diagnosis?" The fact that the headline had to be posed as a question — rather than stated as a conclusion — tells you something important. AMIE's benchmark wins and DXplain's real-world edge are not contradictory. They are measuring different things. The distance between simulation performance and clinical translation is exactly where the remaining work lives.

The Algorithm's Blind Spot

The dermatology number is the one that should not be acceptable to anyone deploying AI in clinical settings. State-of-the-art AI dermatology tools achieve precision of 0.897 on lighter skin phototypes. On darker skin phototypes, precision falls to 0.484. That is not a marginal disparity — it is nearly half the accuracy, applied to patients who are already more likely to have skin conditions diagnosed late by human clinicians operating within a system not designed around their presentation. The cause is documented and straightforward: dermatology AI training datasets contain dark skin tone images in only 4 to 18 percent of total images. The algorithm learned from a dataset that didn't include the full range of human skin. It is now deployed on that full range.

Dermatology is the clearest documented case, but it is not alone. A health management algorithm widely used in U.S. healthcare used prior healthcare spending as a proxy for health need — which systematically underestimated the needs of Black patients, who spend less on healthcare due to access barriers, not superior health. Sepsis prediction models showed significantly reduced accuracy among Hispanic patients due to training data imbalance. These are not theoretical disparities. They are measured failures in deployed systems.

Denial by Algorithm. AdventHealth Shawnee Mission Hospital filed suit against Blue Cross and Blue Shield of Kansas City alleging that its AI-driven claims review system wrongfully denied hundreds of physician-approved diagnoses, withholding more than $2 million in payments — with appeals reportedly denied instantly, without human review. The case introduces a distinct category of AI harm: not a diagnostic error, but an operational one with clinical consequences. Denial-by-algorithm falls hardest on patients least equipped to navigate appeals — and it sits in a legal vacuum where no clear framework governs who is responsible when an automated payer system overrides a physician's clinical judgment.

The pediatric and global gaps compound the picture. Fewer than one in five FDA-cleared AI medical devices are approved for pediatric use — a gap with no clinical justification beyond the economics of validation studies that recruit adults because adults are easier to enroll. Globally, China accounts for 35.6% of AI clinical trial registrations. The United States accounts for 8.5%. Africa and Latin America together represent a fraction of that. Clinical AI is being built by the world's wealthiest health systems, tested primarily on their populations, and then offered — or sold — to everyone else.

The 2026 regulatory environment has made equity harder to enforce, not easier. The Trump administration's deregulatory pivot eliminated Biden-era algorithmic fairness mandates in healthcare, replacing them with "minimally burdensome" requirements that give developers wide latitude over what their tools are tested against. The FDA's January 2026 guidance is silent on demographic representation requirements. There is a bitter irony in the patient trust data: surveys consistently show that communities historically underserved by medicine are among the most willing to give AI a chance — because the alternative they have lived is a human system that hasn't served them well either. That trust deserves better than it is currently receiving.

Accountability Without a Framework

When the algorithm is wrong and a patient is harmed, the current U.S. legal framework has an answer: the physician is liable. No federal law shifts malpractice exposure from the clinician to the AI developer or the hospital that deployed the tool. Courts hold physicians to an independent standard of care regardless of what an algorithm recommended. A doctor who followed an AI output in good faith is still the doctor who signed the note, made the call, and carries the legal exposure for what followed.

The AMA's 2026 survey found that 85% of physicians want direct involvement in AI adoption decisions. When asked what single regulatory action would most build their trust in AI, physicians ranked clear liability frameworks first — above accuracy transparency, data governance requirements, and explainability mandates. The field has developed a phrase for the structural problem they are describing: "liability sink." Clinicians absorb legal exposure for recommendations from black-box systems they cannot audit, trained on data they cannot examine, deployed by institutions that chose the tool without them.

The Ambient AI Calculus. Ambient AI scribes — tools like Nuance DAX Copilot that listen to patient encounters and draft clinical notes — are the fastest-adopted AI in medicine. Marketing claims promise 50% reductions in documentation time. NEJM AI randomized controlled trials published in 2025 and 2026 found a 1.7% documentation time reduction — not statistically significant. What the RCT evidence does show, more robustly: genuine improvement in physician burnout scores on validated instruments. The physician still signs the note and still carries the liability for what it contains. But they arrive at that note less depleted. In a system where physician burnout is itself a patient safety issue, that is not a trivial outcome — even if the time math doesn't pencil out the way the marketing says it does.

The honest state of the field is this: AI demonstrably improves specific, narrow, well-validated clinical tasks. It does not yet reliably improve the full clinical reasoning workflow that constitutes medicine. The stroke neurologist with AI triage is saving lives, measurably, in minutes and neurons. The cardiac electrophysiologist with ECG-AI is catching amyloidosis before the damage is irreversible. The oncologist whose patient lived 13.2 months instead of 6.7 months has a drug in hand that AI helped identify against a cancer that had resisted everything else. These are not abstractions. They are outcomes, in specific patients, in specific clinics.

At the same time, the dermatologist whose AI tool is half as accurate on dark skin may not know it — because the tool doesn't declare its own demographic limitations at the point of use. The patient denied coverage by an automated payer system has no recourse in a legal framework that wasn't designed for this situation. The physician relying on an LLM's differential failed that task, in rigorous testing, more than four times out of five.

Eren Ogut's systematic review of 150 AI clinical medicine studies reached a conclusion that is not pessimistic — just precise: "Current evidence for tangible improvements in day-to-day clinical practice is mixed." That sentence should accompany every AI deployment decision in medicine in 2026. Not a condemnation. A calibration. The field has earned genuine optimism in narrow, validated domains. It has not yet earned the confidence that comes from broad, equitable deployment at scale. The question to carry forward isn't whether AI belongs in the clinic. It does. The question is whether the institutions deploying these systems are honest enough to know what they don't know — and whether the patients least likely to be in the training data will be protected while the field finishes figuring it out.

Glossary

AUC (Area Under the Curve) ↑ Back to text

A measure of a diagnostic test's ability to distinguish positive from negative cases across all possible decision thresholds. An AUC of 1.0 is perfect; 0.5 is random chance. For AI diagnostic tools, AUC is the standard single-number summary of performance — values above 0.9 are considered excellent, and lung nodule detection AI achieving 0.94 approaches the upper range of what any validated clinical test achieves.

Skin Phototype ↑ Back to text

A classification system (the Fitzpatrick scale, Types I–VI) categorizing human skin by its response to UV exposure — from Type I (very light, always burns) to Type VI (deeply pigmented, never burns). In dermatology AI research, phototype is the standard variable for measuring performance disparities across skin tones. Most AI training datasets are heavily skewed toward lighter phototypes (I–III), producing tools with substantially lower accuracy on darker phototypes (IV–VI) — the origin of the 0.897 vs. 0.484 precision gap documented in peer-reviewed literature.

510(k) Clearance

The FDA's premarket notification pathway for devices "substantially equivalent" to an already-marketed product. It is faster than full Premarket Approval — which requires independent clinical evidence — and has become the dominant route for AI medical devices. Ninety-six percent of AI clearances in 2025 used this pathway, raising questions about whether a regulatory shortcut designed for incremental hardware is adequate oversight for software that can behave in novel, unpredictable ways.

Differential Diagnosis

The process of generating a ranked list of possible conditions that could explain a patient's symptoms, then systematically ruling them in or out through testing and observation. It is the central cognitive task of clinical medicine — the structured uncertainty that precedes the answer, not the answer itself. AI systems in 2026 can name a correct final diagnosis with impressive accuracy when data is complete. What they consistently fail at, in rigorous testing, is the differential: the open-ended reasoning at the start of a case.

Alarm Fatigue

The desensitization that sets in when clinical staff receive too many monitoring alerts with high false-positive rates. Clinicians who experience persistent low-signal alerting begin to delay or ignore alerts that matter. In AI-driven sepsis prediction — where systems can flag risk hours before conventional signs appear — alarm fatigue is a documented failure mode that can eliminate the clinical benefit of a statistically strong tool without anyone changing the code.

Liability Sink

A term from healthcare AI ethics describing the structural position of clinicians who absorb full legal and professional exposure for decisions influenced by AI systems they cannot audit and did not choose. U.S. malpractice law holds physicians to an independent standard of care regardless of algorithmic input — meaning physicians carry responsibility for both following and not following AI recommendations, while typically having no legal recourse when an algorithm fails and causes harm.

PCCP (Predetermined Change Control Plan)

An FDA mechanism allowing AI medical device manufacturers to specify in advance what algorithm modifications are permissible without requiring a new regulatory submission. Designed to accommodate adaptive AI. Only 10% of 2025 AI clearances included a PCCP — meaning the vast majority of cleared AI tools cannot update their algorithms without new regulatory review, effectively freezing them at the version that earned clearance, regardless of what the data says afterward.

Ambient AI Scribe

An AI system that passively listens to a clinical encounter — physician and patient conversation — and automatically generates a structured clinical note, eliminating much of the manual documentation work that occupies a large portion of physician time. Tools like Nuance DAX Copilot represent the fastest-adopted category of AI in medicine in 2025–2026. Marketing claims substantially exceed what randomized controlled trial evidence supports in time savings, though burnout improvements have RCT backing.
