After the Curtain Falls:
Media, Learning, and the Age of AI-Generated Everything

JS
Joselito Sering
Editor-in-Chief · AIMA
Published
May 16, 2026
Read Time
18 min read
Category
Media & Society

Something quietly crossed a threshold in 2025. An Ahrefs analysis of nearly one million new web pages found that 74.2 percent contained detectable AI-generated content. Nine of the top 100 fastest-growing YouTube channels operated entirely on AI-produced media. Meanwhile, the generative AI market in media and entertainment — valued at $19 billion in 2024 — is on track to reach $153 billion by 2033. We are not standing at the edge of change. We have already crossed into it. The question now is not whether AI will transform how humans create, consume, and learn from media. The question is what kind of transformation this will be — and what choices we must make, collectively, to ensure it serves human flourishing.

The Signal Was Always There

To understand where we are going, it helps to understand who saw this coming — and how long ago. The futurists of the early AI era were, in many cases, strikingly precise.

Ray Kurzweil, computer scientist, inventor, and author of The Age of Spiritual Machines (1999) and The Singularity Is Near (2005), built an intellectual career on the "law of accelerating returns" — the observation that fundamental measures of information technology follow predictable exponential trajectories regardless of war, recession, or political disruption. Among the predictions he made in the 1990s for the year 2009: "Digital objects such as books, music albums, movies, and software are rapidly distributed as data files through the wireless network, and typically do not have a physical object associated with them." That came true within a year of his target — Apple's iTunes Store became the top US music distributor in 2008. He also predicted, in 1999, that artificial general intelligence would reach human-level capability by 2029. His overall predictive accuracy has been independently assessed at approximately 86 percent.

Nicholas Negroponte, co-founder of the MIT Media Lab and author of Being Digital (1995), took a different approach. He called his forecasts "extrapolations, not predictions" — an important distinction. At the inaugural TED conference in 1984, he correctly described the future of streaming entertainment, touchscreen interfaces, e-books, and personalized news feeds — decades before any of them existed commercially. His core thesis, that the world was undergoing a shift from atoms to bits, turned out to be the defining frame of the digital age. In a 2025 retrospective interview, he noted: "We now live, work and play in a digital age — and like air and drinking water, being digital will only be noticed in its absence." His later prediction — that knowledge might one day be delivered to the brain through biotechnology — remains speculative but is now directionally supported by advances in brain-computer interfaces.

🎙
Kurzweil's 2006 EDUCAUSE Address
"We will be able to expand our intelligence a thousandfold by the 2040s. The biological and non-biological thinking will be indistinguishable from one another. All of this will happen in 25 years." Speaking to educators in 2006, Kurzweil described a future in which learning would be available anywhere, anytime — and in which AI would fundamentally alter not just what we know, but how we come to know it. Nineteen years later, his words read less like prophecy and more like a field report from the present.

The early-AI futurists shared a structural belief: that the exponential nature of computing would eventually reach a point where the difference between "assisted creation" and "autonomous creation" would become indistinguishable to the average consumer. We are now living at that inflection point. What the futurists did not fully model — because the social science wasn't yet available — was the profound asymmetry between adoption speed and human readiness. Technology moves in code. Culture moves in generations.

The Sound Machine: AI and the Music Industry

Music was the first major creative domain to fully absorb AI-generated output into mainstream consumption, and the numbers are significant. According to market research firm The Business Research Company, the AI music market reached $4.48 billion in 2025 and is projected to grow to $12.86 billion by 2030 at a compound annual growth rate of 23.4 percent. Platforms like Suno, Udio, AIVA, and Soundraw have moved from novelty to workflow staple in under three years.

The adoption data is striking in its breadth. Approximately 60 percent of musicians and producers now incorporate AI into their creative processes. Electronic music leads AI adoption at 54 percent, followed closely by hip-hop at 53 percent. Over 40 percent of global music listeners regularly engage with AI-generated tracks — often without knowing it. AI-powered recommendation systems already drive more than 80 percent of content discovery on major streaming platforms.

But perhaps the most significant data point is not about creation — it is about perception. Approximately 35 percent of listeners still prefer human-created music, citing authenticity and emotional depth as critical differentiators. This is the tension that will define the next decade: not whether AI can produce music that sounds indistinguishable from human work, but whether listeners will come to value the distinction itself.

"Premium, authentic, human-led content will be even more valuable if the overall supply of content increases."
— Alexandra Shannon, Executive, Creative Artists Agency (CAA), quoted in McKinsey, 2026

The intellectual property dimension is already contentious. In September 2025, Sweden's music rights organisation STIM launched what may be the world's first AI music license — allowing AI companies to legally train on copyrighted songs, while guaranteeing compensation to songwriters and composers who opt in. Meanwhile, approximately 45 percent of industry stakeholders cite copyright ambiguity as a major challenge, and litigation between major studios and AI firms over training data continues to escalate.

Experts project that by 2030, nearly 80 percent of new music projects will involve some form of AI assistance. The more urgent question is not whether AI will dominate music production — it likely will — but whether the economic models can be restructured to ensure that the musicians who trained those systems, willingly or not, receive fair compensation for the culture they contributed.

💡
The Homogenization Risk
A 2023 paper from UCL School of Management and the University of Exeter found that generative AI enhances individual creativity but reduces the diversity of novel content at a population level. When everyone uses the same model, trained on the same corpus, the range of cultural expression may narrow even as the volume of content explodes. This is not a reason to reject AI music tools — it is a reason to build them with diversity as an explicit design value.

Cinema Without a Camera

Film and television are experiencing a more complex disruption than music, partly because the production pipeline is longer and more capital-intensive, and partly because the human collaboration at its center — between writers, directors, cinematographers, and performers — has historically been its defining creative currency.

McKinsey's January 2026 report on AI in film and TV, based on interviews with over twenty studio executives, producers, AI innovators, and academics, offers the most rigorous current assessment. The report identifies three potential industry trajectories: a scenario in which AI enhances existing workflows without structural disruption; a restructured model in which creative boundaries are redrawn and value pools redistributed; and a fundamental reset of the entire video production landscape that changes the economic model from its foundation.

McKinsey estimates that approximately $10 billion of forecast US original content spend in 2030 could be addressable by some form of AI. More provocatively, it projects that around $60 billion in revenues could be redistributed within five years of reaching mass adoption — drawing on historical patterns showing that major technology shifts from stage plays to cinema, and from linear to streaming, resulted in an average 35 percent contraction of incumbent revenue in the five years following widespread adoption.

Production executives describe the impact as structural. "I looked at every step of the workflow from ideation to distribution," said Sean Bailey, founder of B5 Studios, to McKinsey researchers, "and I really think every single piece of it will be significantly disrupted." At the same time, leaders emphasize a distinction that is both commercial and ethical: human-led storytelling is being positioned as a quality signal, not a quaint tradition. "What is most exciting about AI," said Lori McCreary, CEO of Revelations Entertainment, "is the potential to expand our human imagination and get to a point in storytelling where we don't have to be thinking: Can I shoot that on a film set?"

A 2025 paper published in AI and Ethics (Springer) provides an academic counterweight to the commercial optimism. Analyzing case studies across film, television, and game production, the researchers found that while AI accelerates workflows and expands creative possibilities, its adoption risks making certain creative tasks redundant, and raises substantive concerns about originality and creative homogenization. The paper proposes hybrid workflows — in which AI handles repetitive tasks while humans focus on ideation and refinement — as the most ethically sustainable model.

⚠️
Authorship and Labor
The ongoing legal battles between Hollywood studios and AI firms over training data, and between creative unions and studios over AI use in production, are not simply contractual disputes. They are negotiations over a fundamental question: when an AI system learns from thousands of human performances, scripts, and compositions to generate new work, who is the author? Current law does not have a satisfying answer. The policy choices made in the next three to five years will shape the creative economy for decades.

Books Without Authors: The Written Word in Transition

The written word was, in some ways, the most vulnerable creative domain — and the one that moved fastest. As of April 2025, Ahrefs analysis found that 74.2 percent of newly published web pages contained detectable AI-generated content. In academic peer review, a 2025 study from the University of Pennsylvania found that approximately 20 percent of ICLR conference reviews and 12 percent of Nature Communications reviews were classified as AI-generated, with the trend accelerating sharply through 2024. More than 50 percent of surveyed academics reported using AI tools during peer review, even when core judgments remained human-authored.

For books specifically, the disruption is accelerating across three distinct channels: AI-assisted writing tools that help human authors work faster; fully AI-generated content flooding e-book marketplaces (particularly genre fiction); and AI-powered summarization and synthesis tools that change how readers engage with long-form text. Amazon's Kindle marketplace began grappling with floods of AI-generated books as early as 2023, and the volume has only increased.

The cultural consequence is a paradox: we may be entering an era of unprecedented textual abundance and simultaneous reading scarcity. When anyone can generate a competent novel in hours, the value proposition of a book shifts from "what is written" to "who wrote it and why." The signal that survives is authorship as a form of relationship — the reader trusting not just the content, but the consciousness behind it.

Negroponte's 1995 framework anticipated this tension when he wrote about the shift from mass media to personalized media. "The daily me," as he called it — a news feed perfectly tailored to individual interests — is now a reality. But the downstream effect he did not fully model is that personalization, taken to its logical extreme, can become an echo chamber. When AI generates content specifically calibrated to your existing beliefs and preferences, the discovery of uncomfortable, disruptive, or transformative ideas becomes structurally less likely.

✓
The Case for Human Authorship
There is strong evidence that readers respond differently to work they know was created by a living person with stakes in the world. Studies on parasocial relationships in media consumption consistently show that the perceived humanity of a creator — their vulnerability, their specific lived experience, their willingness to be wrong — is a significant driver of deep engagement. AI can produce technically excellent prose. It cannot, as of 2026, produce genuine moral courage, lived grief, or earned wisdom. Those remain distinctly human offerings.

Learning, Reimagined: AI in Education and Human Development

If entertainment is the domain most visible in the AI disruption, education is the domain with the highest stakes. How we learn determines who we become. And the transformation underway in learning is both more promising and more uncertain than most accounts acknowledge.

UNESCO's September 2025 Digital Learning Week — convening over 400 global leaders, educators, learners, policymakers, and civil society representatives in Paris under the theme "AI and the Future of Education: Disruptions, Dilemmas and Directions" — produced a landmark assessment. The report concluded that AI offers genuine opportunities for broader access, personalized learning, and more effective educational management. It simultaneously warned that without critical reflection, AI could deepen inequalities, threaten academic integrity, and weaken the human relationships at the heart of learning.

The evidence for AI's positive educational impact is meaningful. A 2025 systematic review of AI-driven intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) in K-12 education, synthesizing 28 quasi-experimental studies covering 4,597 students, found generally positive effects on learning outcomes compared to conventional methods — with particularly strong gains when systems provided real-time progress information and encouraged student reflection. A 2026 assessment from the Educational Technology and Change Journal, drawing on multiple systematic reviews and UNESCO and OECD policy guidance, identified AI-driven personalized tutoring, AI-supported formative assessment, and AI as a teacher workload-reduction tool as the three clearest cases where AI capabilities align with robust evidence of learning gains.

The equity dimension is not a footnote — it is the central challenge. As of 2024, nearly one-third of the world's population, approximately 2.6 billion people, still lacks internet access. Without deliberate policy intervention, the AI education revolution risks creating a new kind of intellectual caste system: those with access to AI tutoring that adapts to their pace, language, and learning style, and those without. The UNESCO report explicitly frames this as a rights issue, arguing for a human-centered, rights-based approach that ensures AI innovations strengthen learning opportunities for all rather than leaving anyone behind.

Negroponte's vision for education — that the best learning happens when children are given tools to think with, not facts to memorize — finds its most powerful contemporary expression in well-designed AI tutoring. His founding motto at the MIT Media Lab, mens et manus (mind and hand), is arguably more achievable now than at any previous point in history: AI can lower the barrier to making things, experimenting, and discovering through doing. The risk is that poorly designed systems reverse this — reducing learning to prompt-and-response exchanges that look like learning but bypass the productive struggle that actually builds understanding.

"The future of education will be shaped by the policy choices that we make about education investment, about pedagogy, about governance, and about the ethical principles we uphold."
— UNESCO Digital Learning Week 2025, Paris

Perhaps the most profound shift in learning is not about tools at all — it is about what knowledge is in a world where AI can retrieve, synthesize, and explain almost anything on demand. The traditional educational model — transfer knowledge from expert to student, assess retention — becomes inadequate when the student has a pocket-sized system capable of outperforming most human experts on recall tasks. What becomes valuable is not knowing facts, but knowing which questions to ask, how to evaluate competing claims, and how to act wisely in the face of uncertainty. These are, arguably, the oldest educational goals of all.

Probable Scenarios: Looking Ahead

What follows is not prediction in the Kurzweil sense — a confident forecast grounded in observed exponential curves. It is scenario planning: an honest attempt to map the space of plausible futures based on current data, expert consensus, and the structural forces at work. Three time horizons matter: the near term (2027), the medium term (2031), and the long term (2036–2046).

The Next Two Years (2027)

Most ProbableHybrid Abundance with Authentic Scarcity
AI-generated content dominates volume across music, film (short-form), books, and educational materials. The sheer quantity of content triggers a market correction in attention economics: human-verified, clearly-attributed work commands a premium. Major platforms introduce "human-made" labels, driven partly by regulation and partly by consumer demand. In education, AI tutoring becomes standard in well-funded school systems; the gap with under-resourced systems widens. Labor disputes in creative industries are partially resolved through hybrid licensing models similar to STIM's 2025 AI music license. Copyright law in the US and EU begins to differentiate between AI training (permissible with compensation) and AI output (subject to disclosure requirements).

Five Years (2031)

ContestedThe Great Stratification
McKinsey's $60 billion revenue redistribution begins in earnest. Incumbent studios that failed to adapt have consolidated or dissolved; new AI-native production companies dominate short-form and mid-budget content. Live performance and provably-human creative work experience a renaissance — driven by the same scarcity logic that sustains artisan crafts in an industrial economy. In education, AI tutoring systems are standard in OECD countries; pilot programs in Southeast Asia, Africa, and Latin America show measurable gains but require sustained policy investment and local cultural adaptation to succeed. The UNESCO warning about AI deepening inequality proves partially correct: the gains are real, but unevenly distributed. AGI has not arrived, but AI systems are performing at or above human expert level in an expanding range of knowledge tasks. The nature of human expertise is being redefined in real time.

Ten Years (2036)

SpeculativeThe Authorship Economy
If Kurzweil's 2029 AGI timeline holds — and there are credible arguments that it is directionally correct, if not precisely dated — by 2036 we are operating in a post-AGI transitional period. Entertainment has bifurcated entirely: an AI-generated content layer of effectively infinite volume, personalized to individual tastes with uncanny precision; and a human-made layer that is rare, expensive, and culturally prestigious. The analogy is not film versus theater — it is mass-produced wine versus single-origin wine from a specific vineyard and a specific year. Both exist, both have markets, but they serve different human needs. In education, the Kurzweil vision of "learning anytime, anywhere" is fully realized in the material sense; the question that occupies educators and policymakers is whether the resulting knowledge is producing wisdom, civic virtue, and human connection — or sophisticated consumers of AI-generated experience.

Twenty Years (2046)

Long HorizonThe Integration Question
Kurzweil's singularity hypothesis places 2045 as the point at which AI surpasses the combined intelligence of all humans. Whether or not this specific milestone is reached, the direction of travel is toward a world in which the boundary between human cognition and computational assistance becomes increasingly porous — through brain-computer interfaces, ambient AI, and possibly the biotechnological learning mechanisms that Negroponte speculated about in the 2010s. Entertainment in this horizon may not be a category at all — the distinction between "consuming content" and "living experience" may dissolve in immersive, AI-mediated environments. What survives — what will always be worth seeking — is the irreducible fact of another consciousness: a person who struggled, loved, failed, and made something from it. The most enduring creative works have always been acts of testimony. That will not change.

What Remains Human

There is a temptation, when surveying the scale of AI's capabilities, to conclude that the human role in media and learning is being methodically reduced. That conclusion is both premature and structurally incorrect.

Every major communication technology in history — the printing press, the telegraph, radio, cinema, television, the internet — was greeted with a version of the same anxiety: that the new medium would hollow out human connection, produce passive consumers, and diminish what made culture valuable. In each case, the anxiety was partially warranted and partially mistaken. The printing press did displace certain kinds of scribal labor; it also democratized literacy. Television did fragment some forms of community gathering; it also created shared cultural experiences at a scale previously impossible. The internet did accelerate misinformation; it also gave voice to billions who had none.

AI-generated media is different in degree but not necessarily in kind. Its distinguishing feature is the first-time elimination of skill as the primary barrier to creation. Previously, the gap between "having something to say" and "being able to say it compellingly" was filled by years of craft development — in music, in filmmaking, in writing. AI collapses that gap. This is liberating for the billions of people who have something genuine to express and lacked the technical means to express it. It is disorienting for the professionals whose value was partly rooted in that skill gap.

The UNESCO 2025 framework offers a useful principle for navigating this: the future of education with AI "is not about replacing teachers but empowering them to lead with humanity in a world where technology is everywhere." The same logic applies to all creative domains. The question is not whether AI will participate in the making of culture — it already does, at scale. The question is what role human judgment, human experience, and human accountability play in that process. If AI is deployed as a cost-reduction tool with no human creative stake in the outcome, the results will be culturally thin. If AI is deployed as a creative amplifier — expanding what a committed human creator can make and who can make it — the results could be genuinely extraordinary.

✓
A Note on Adaptation
Every prior generation of media creators adapted or became obsolete. Typographers adapted when desktop publishing arrived. Film editors adapted when nonlinear digital editing arrived. Photographers adapted when digital cameras arrived. In each case, the professionals who thrived were those who understood both the technology and the human purpose it served. The current generation of musicians, filmmakers, writers, and educators faces the same choice — with a compressed timeline and higher stakes.

For communities like the Philippines and the broader Southeast Asian region, the AI media transformation carries particular promise and particular risk. The promise: a world in which geographic and economic distance no longer determines access to great creative tools or great educational resources. Filipino musicians, filmmakers, and educators can now compete on equal technical footing with peers in New York, London, or Seoul. The risk: if the dominant AI systems are trained primarily on English-language, Western-cultural data, they will export a narrow slice of human experience as if it were universal. Building culturally diverse, multilingual AI systems — and insisting on local data sovereignty — is not a luxury. It is a prerequisite for a genuinely human AI future.

The curtain has fallen on the old media order. What rises in its place is not yet determined. That indeterminacy is not a cause for despair. It is an invitation — to be deliberate, to be creative, and to insist that the technology serves the deepest purposes of human culture: to help us understand ourselves, to connect us to one another, and to expand what we believe is possible.

Glossary

Generative AI

A class of artificial intelligence systems capable of producing new content — text, images, audio, video, or code — based on patterns learned from large training datasets. Distinguished from earlier AI by its ability to create original output rather than merely classify or predict from existing data. Examples include large language models (GPT-4, Claude), image generators (Midjourney, DALL·E), and music generators (Suno, AIVA).

↑ Back to text
Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)

A hypothetical form of AI capable of performing any intellectual task that a human can perform, with comparable or superior efficiency across all domains — not just the narrow tasks that current AI systems excel at. Ray Kurzweil has predicted AGI will arrive by 2029. The definition remains contested: some researchers argue AGI requires consciousness or general-purpose physical embodiment; others define it functionally as human-level performance on a broad benchmark suite.

↑ Back to text
AI Music Market

The commercial ecosystem of products, platforms, and services that use artificial intelligence to compose, produce, distribute, license, or recommend music. As of 2025, the market is valued at approximately $4.48 billion globally and is growing at a compound annual growth rate of 23.4 percent. Key players include Suno, Udio, AIVA, Soundraw, Amper Music, and AI recommendation engines embedded in Spotify, Apple Music, and YouTube Music.

↑ Back to text
Creative Homogenization

A cultural process in which the diversity of creative output decreases as creators converge on similar tools, training datasets, or aesthetic norms. In the context of AI-generated media, homogenization risk arises when large numbers of creators use the same generative model — which was itself trained on a particular (and non-representative) corpus of human culture — producing outputs that are individually varied but collectively similar in structure, aesthetic, and cultural perspective.

↑ Back to text
Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS)

AI-powered educational software that adapts its instruction to the individual learner's pace, knowledge state, and learning style in real time. ITS systems provide personalized feedback, adjust difficulty, and identify gaps in understanding without requiring a human teacher to be present. Evidence from a 2025 systematic review of 28 quasi-experimental studies involving 4,597 K-12 students found consistently positive effects on learning outcomes compared to conventional instruction.

↑ Back to text
Law of Accelerating Returns

A theory proposed by Ray Kurzweil in The Age of Spiritual Machines (1999) and developed further in subsequent works. The law states that fundamental measures of information technology — processing speed, memory capacity, cost per computation — follow predictable exponential growth trajectories that are not disrupted by economic downturns, wars, or political instability. Kurzweil uses this framework to argue that AI capabilities will continue accelerating at an increasing rate, making long-range technological forecasting possible through pattern recognition rather than speculation.

Atoms to Bits

Nicholas Negroponte's foundational metaphor from Being Digital (1995) describing the shift from physical media — vinyl records, printed books, film canisters — to digital information that can be copied, transmitted, and reproduced at near-zero marginal cost. Negroponte argued that once culture became "bits," the economics, distribution, and consumption patterns of media would be fundamentally transformed. The metaphor remains useful for understanding the ongoing transition from AI-assisted to AI-generated content.

Digital Divide / AI Divide

The gap between populations that have access to digital technologies and those that do not. As of 2024, approximately 2.6 billion people — one-third of the global population — lack internet access. UNESCO warns that without deliberate policy intervention, the rapid expansion of AI-powered education and media tools risks creating an "AI divide" that further disadvantages vulnerable groups, including girls, rural populations, people with disabilities, and communities in the Global South.
