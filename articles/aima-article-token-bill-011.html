The Token Bill: How AI Overspending Is Rewriting the Future of Work

JS
Joselito Sering
Editor-in-Chief · AIMA
Published
June 1, 2026
Read Time
11 min read
Category
Industry Analysis

Somewhere in 2026, the math stopped making sense. Tech companies laid off over 142,000 workers in five months, cited AI displacement as a primary cause, and then quietly discovered a new expense dwarfing what they had saved: the token bill. The promise was efficiency. The reality, for many enterprises, is a cost structure as unmanageable as the payroll they dismantled. This article examines whether the claim that companies now spend more on AI tokens than on the workers they replaced is supported by evidence — and what that reckoning means for the millions of people watching from the other side of the equation.

Fact-Checking the Claim

The assertion that enterprises spend more on inference tokens than on the employees they replaced is not a provable universal truth — but it is increasingly, verifiably true at specific companies, and the pattern is spreading.

The most direct evidence comes from Uber. Its Chief Technology Officer, Praveen Neppalli Naga, admitted that the company’s reliance on Anthropic’s Claude Code had exhausted its entire 2026 AI budget within the first four months of the year. Separately, Nvidia Vice President Bryan Catanzaro stated publicly that within his team, the cost of AI compute now exceeds the cost of the employees using it. GetSwan CEO Amos Bar-Joseph shared that his four-person team spent more than $113,000 on AI tools in a single month. Max Linder, a software engineer in Stockholm, told The New York Times that he likely spends more on Claude than he earns in salary.

⚠
The Tokenmaxxing Problem
Meta created internal leaderboards that ranked employees by token consumption. Amazon reportedly encouraged staff to use as many AI tokens as possible. The resulting behavior — dubbed “tokenmaxxing” — saw some individual users generating monthly token bills exceeding $150,000. The engineering analytics firm Faros AI found that code churn (lines of code deleted versus lines added) increased by more than 800% under high AI adoption, suggesting significant waste embedded in aggressive usage.

A 2018 MIT study — still widely cited — concluded that AI automation is economically viable in only about 23% of jobs. That means human workers remain cheaper in the remaining 77%. Short-sighted enterprises that replaced people with AI agents could face bills five times higher than original payroll costs at scale, because agents operate continuously without breaks, accruing token charges around the clock. Goldman Sachs has forecast that agentic AI could drive a 24-fold increase in token consumption by 2030, reaching 120 quadrillion tokens per month globally — even as the per-token price falls.

◆
Gartner’s Warning
Gartner Senior Director Analyst Will Sommer put it plainly: “Chief Product Officers should not confuse the deflation of commodity tokens with the democratization of frontier reasoning.” Cheaper tokens do not mean cheaper enterprise AI. Agentic models require far more tokens per task than standard models, and AI providers are unlikely to pass through lower infrastructure costs entirely to consumers. Inference costs are projected to push higher, not lower, in real enterprise deployments.

The claim is therefore partly true, demonstrably true at the most aggressive adopters, and directionally true as agentic adoption scales. It is not universally true, and it is not permanent. Token prices will fall. But the pattern of unmanaged consumption replacing one kind of cost pressure with another is now confirmed at the executive level.

The Layoff Wave: What the Numbers Actually Show

The scale of the 2026 tech layoff wave exceeds every comparable cycle since the post-pandemic correction of 2022–2023. More than 142,000 tech jobs were eliminated in the first five months of 2026 alone — a 33% increase over the same period in 2025. What distinguishes this cycle from prior ones is not the volume, but the context: the same companies executing the deepest cuts were simultaneously posting record revenues and committing to the largest infrastructure buildout in tech history.

Amazon eliminated approximately 16,000 corporate roles in Q1 2026, more than half of all tech layoffs in the quarter, while reporting AWS growth of 24% — its fastest in 13 quarters. Oracle cut an estimated 20,000 to 30,000 positions, roughly 20% of its global workforce, immediately after reporting strong quarterly earnings. Meta announced 8,000 cuts (10% of its workforce) effective May 20, the same day Intuit announced the elimination of 3,000 roles representing 17% of its global headcount. Block CEO Jack Dorsey stated explicitly that the 4,000 jobs eliminated in March 2026 — the largest single AI-attributed layoff in corporate history — were not driven by financial difficulty but by the growing capability of AI tools.

📊
The 2026 Layoff Snapshot
142,000+ tech jobs cut in five months (Jan–May 2026) · 33% higher pace than 2025 · ~48% of tracked cuts explicitly attributed to AI or automation · $700B combined capex committed by Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, and Meta for 2026 — nearly double 2025 spending · 17 of 28 companies announcing AI-related layoffs saw their stock prices rise on the day of the announcement.

Stanford Human-AI Institute data documents that software developer employment among workers under 26 fell nearly 20% since 2024 — a concentrated impact on entry-level roles that reflects AI adoption moving fastest where tasks are most structured and verifiable. Challenger, Gray & Christmas confirmed that AI was the leading stated cause of layoffs in April 2026 for the second consecutive month, accounting for 26% of all cuts that month.

⚠
AI Washing Is Real
OpenAI CEO Sam Altman acknowledged publicly that some companies are engaged in what he called “AI washing” — blaming AI for layoffs that would have happened anyway due to poor financial performance or geopolitical pressures. Deutsche Bank analysts wrote in January 2026 that “AI redundancy washing will be a significant feature of 2026.” A Gartner survey of 350 global executives found that 80% of companies that piloted AI or autonomous technology reported workforce reductions — regardless of whether the technology was generating actual returns. Oxford Economics concluded in January 2026 that firms “don’t appear to be replacing workers with AI on a significant scale” when measured against macroeconomic employment data. The truth is layered: some displacement is real, some is cover for routine restructuring, and from the outside, the two are nearly indistinguishable.

Which Industries Are Truly Disrupted

The disruption is not uniform. It concentrates in roles where tasks are predictable, repetitive, and measurable — and it is moving through industries in a sequence that tracks those characteristics.

Customer Support and Service

Customer support is the most heavily and immediately disrupted category. AI chatbots and virtual assistants now manage up to 80% of all routine service interactions. Block’s 4,000 layoffs were concentrated here, as the company’s AI systems demonstrated the ability to resolve 70–80% of customer inquiries without human intervention. Similar patterns have played out at eBay, Pinterest, and others. IBM’s AskHR system handles 11.5 million interactions annually with less than 5% human oversight. Across the sector, call centers may see a 50% workforce reduction by 2035. This is not a projection; the leading edge of it is measurable today.

Software Development and IT

Deutsche Bank’s AI analysis found that over 85% of developers now use AI coding assistants, with companies reporting productivity gains of up to 60%. Those gains are real. They are also eliminating the pipeline. Stanford HAI data confirms entry-level engineering roles under 26 have contracted sharply. Anthropic has stated that close to 100% of its code is now AI-generated; Google and Microsoft report approximately 25%. The roles most at risk are junior developers whose primary function was writing routine, well-defined code — the same code now handled by tools like Claude Code and GitHub Copilot. Senior engineers with architectural judgment are not equivalently threatened. The gap between the two is widening.

Finance, Insurance, and Legal

Deutsche Bank identifies the financial sector as ripe for disruption, with wealth management particularly vulnerable. An estimated 80% of retail investors will depend primarily on AI tools for advice by 2027. Banks are deploying AI across accounting, auditing, fraud detection, customer service, and data processing. Law firm Baker McKenzie eliminated 600–1,000 positions in early 2026, primarily targeting support staff — researchers, marketers, and secretarial roles — as AI absorbed their workload. Research, paralegal work, and document review represent the legal sector’s highest-risk categories.

Creative and Marketing

Content creation and marketing roles represent the second most affected category in tech layoffs. The disruption follows a pattern of bifurcation: commodity creative work (stock photography, template design, basic copywriting, standard video editing) is collapsing in price and volume, while premium strategic and creative roles are growing. AI creative directors, content strategists who can direct AI tools, and senior designers whose output AI cannot replicate are seeing salary premiums of 15–30%. The middle tier of competent but not exceptional creative workers faces the greatest risk.

Administrative and Clerical

An estimated 6.1 million U.S. clerical and administrative workers are at high risk, with manual data-entry roles facing 95% automation risk. These workers have the lowest adaptive capacity. They tend to be older, less digitally connected, and less able to rapidly reskill. The Brookings Institution identifies this group as the most structurally vulnerable because the skills being displaced are not adjacent to the skills being created.

The workers getting squeezed are concentrated in a particular kind of role, at a particular kind of company, in a particular geography. The AI shock is real — but it is not hitting everyone equally.

— Box CEO Aaron Levie, in a16z Podcast, April 2026

The ROI Problem Nobody Wants to Admit

The productivity narrative sustaining the AI investment boom rests on an uncomfortable empirical foundation: at the macroeconomic level, the gains have not yet shown up.

Apollo Global Management Chief Economist Torsten Slok draws an explicit parallel to the 1980s IT boom. Nobel laureate Robert Solow observed in that era that computers were everywhere except in the productivity statistics. Slok sees the same pattern today: “AI is everywhere now, but it is not in economic indicators.” Goldman Sachs released an analysis finding no statistically significant correlation between overall AI adoption rates and productivity at the economy-wide level. Productivity improvements of approximately 30% have been confirmed in specific categories — customer service and software development — but the aggregate signal remains muted.

At the firm level, the picture is sharper but still sobering. A February 2026 study found that over 80% of companies using AI showed no measurable productivity benefit. The IBM Institute for Business Value found that enterprise AI initiatives achieved just 5.9% ROI against a 10% capital investment. A UC Berkeley research team found that 67% of employees reported an increase in working hours after AI adoption, not a decrease. The Harvard Business Review found AI use was increasing worker burnout, not reducing it.

⚠
The Workslop Problem
Stanford and BetterUp researchers identified a growing source of productivity loss they termed “workslop” — low-quality AI-generated content that creates downstream work for recipients. 40% of workers report receiving workslop in the past month. Each incident consumes nearly two hours to correct or redo. For a 10,000-person organization, the annual cost in lost time exceeds $9 million. Only 3 of 10 companies are utilizing AI proportionally to the cost, according to Inven Global’s analysis.

Controlled workplace studies consistently show large productivity gains in specific task categories: writing, customer support, software development, and translation, often improving quality alongside speed. A Procter & Gamble study found that individual employees assisted by AI matched the performance of entire teams without AI on innovation challenges. These gains are real. The disconnect is between what works in controlled conditions and what scales across enterprises with diverse workflows, change management challenges, and legacy systems.

Gartner’s Vice President Helen Poitevin offered the clearest diagnostic: “Chasing value only through headcount reduction is likely to lead most organizations down a path of limited returns.” A Gartner survey confirmed the mechanism — 80% of companies that piloted AI cut workers, but those cuts bore no correlation to higher ROI. The layoffs preceded the productivity gains in many cases, rather than resulting from them. Companies cut people on the expectation of AI replacing their work, not on evidence that it already had.

What Workers Should Actually Worry About

The aggregate labor data is not yet alarming. Jonathan Hartley and colleagues found that 35.9% of U.S. workers used generative AI by December 2025, with no statistically significant declines in job openings or employment in exposed occupations. A Yale Budget Lab report using Bureau of Labor Statistics Current Population Survey data found no significant AI-related labor changes through March 2026. These are real findings, not reassuring spin.

The risk is not evenly distributed, however, and the distribution matters more than the average.

The Entry-Level Problem

The workers most immediately at risk are those entering the workforce in highly exposed sectors. Software developer employment under 26 has fallen nearly 20% since 2024. Customer service, data entry, and administrative roles are contracting at the entry level faster than experienced workers are displaced. A 24-year-old computer science graduate in 2026 faces a market where the junior roles that historically provided the experience base for a technology career are being compressed by tools that can perform the same work on demand.

The Skills Gap Is Real and Widening

AI skills in AI-exposed jobs are changing 66% faster than in other jobs. 77% of employers plan to reskill workers for AI by 2030. Only 13% of employees have received any AI training to date. The hiring market reflects this: companies report a 92% increase in hiring for AI-related positions in 2026, with a 20–40% salary premium for AI proficiency. The problem is structural: the workers being laid off are largely not the workers being hired. The Brookings Institution notes that the most exposed workers also have the lowest adaptive capacity.

The Gender and Race Dimension

Globally, approximately 4.7% of women’s jobs face severe disruption from AI compared to 2.4% of men’s. Women are more concentrated in administrative and office roles with higher AI exposure. Rising unemployment for Black Americans — at 7.5% in 2025 compared to 3.8% for White Americans — reflects the concentration of displacement in sectors and roles where minority workers are overrepresented. These disparities do not emerge from AI itself but from the intersection of AI adoption with existing labor market inequalities.

✓
What Workers Can Do
Anthropic’s January 2026 Economic Index found that 52% of Claude conversations were classified as augmentation (not replacement) — especially in complex knowledge-intensive tasks. The 2026 AI Job Disruption Report is clear: the single biggest career risk is inaction. The strategy is the same regardless of role: understand the technology, build practical skills with AI tools, and position yourself on the augmentation side. A salary premium of 20–40% across industries awaits workers who do this successfully.

The Trajectory: What Comes Next

Three forces will define the next three years.

Token Prices Will Fall. Consumption Will Rise.

Gartner projects that inference on a one-trillion-parameter model will cost 90% less by 2030 than in 2025. That decline is real. But Goldman Sachs projects a 24-fold increase in token consumption over the same period as agentic AI scales. Cheaper tokens do not translate to cheaper enterprise AI when agents run 24 hours a day on every workflow. The aggregate spend trajectory is upward even as unit economics improve.

The Productivity Signal Will Clarify

The Solow Paradox — productivity visible in workplaces but not in statistics — typically resolves on a five-to-ten-year lag as organizational practices adapt to new tools. The Council of Economic Advisers projects that AI could generate growth divergence analogous to earlier industrial transitions, with economies that invest in compute capacity and human-AI collaboration positioned to benefit substantially. The World Economic Forum estimates that approximately 1.1 billion jobs could be transformed (not destroyed) by technology over the next decade, with net positive job creation conditional on reskilling investment.

Policy Is Arriving, Unevenly

California Governor Gavin Newsom signed an executive order in May 2026 directing state agencies to study AI-driven displacement and develop policy recommendations — the first such action at scale in the United States. It is a study, not a protection. The policy response to the 2026 layoff wave has arrived in intention. The practical protections for displaced workers have not.

The World Economic Forum is direct about what the transition requires: human-AI collaboration, agentic workflows, data governance, and infrastructure investment. Organizations that simply layer AI onto existing structures — cutting workers first and asking productivity questions later — are the ones most likely to end up with both an inflated token bill and a less capable organization. The companies building for what comes after the cost panic are investing in both the technology and the people who use it.

The future of work is not something that will simply happen to us. We are actively constructing it, through the choices individuals make, the norms teams build, and the systems organizations adopt.

— Microsoft Research, New Future of Work Report, April 2026

The tech companies spending more on tokens than they were on workers are not in a stable equilibrium. They are in a period of expensive discovery — finding out, at scale, which AI investments generate returns and which generate bills. The workers displaced in that process deserve a clearer answer than the market is currently providing. The honest one, backed by the evidence available today, is this: the displacement is real in concentrated sectors, the productivity gains are real in controlled conditions, and the gap between those two truths is where the hardest work — policy, reskilling, organizational design — still needs to happen.

Glossary

AI Displacement ↑ Back to text

The reduction or elimination of human employment roles as a direct result of artificial intelligence systems becoming capable of performing the same tasks. Displacement differs from augmentation in that it involves net job loss rather than task reallocation. In 2026, AI displacement has been explicitly cited by companies including Block, Amazon, and Oracle as a driver of workforce reductions, though the distinction between genuine displacement and routine restructuring attributed to AI (“AI washing”) remains contested.

Token Cost ↑ Back to text

The financial cost associated with processing text through a large language model (LLM), measured in “tokens” — units roughly corresponding to four characters of text or three-quarters of a word. Enterprises are billed by AI providers based on the number of tokens processed (input) and generated (output) per request. At scale, particularly with agentic AI systems running continuously, token costs can exceed projections significantly and outpace savings from reduced headcount.

Inference (AI) ↑ Back to text

The process by which a trained AI model generates responses or predictions from new input data. Inference is the “running” phase of AI — as opposed to training, which is the learning phase. Enterprise AI costs are dominated by inference, not training, because inference occurs every time a user or automated system sends a request. As models grow larger and agentic workflows multiply the number of requests per task, inference costs compound rapidly.

AI Agents ↑ Back to text

Autonomous AI systems designed to complete multi-step tasks with minimal human supervision, often by calling tools, browsing the web, writing and executing code, and interacting with external services. Unlike a single-turn chatbot interaction, an agent may make dozens or hundreds of LLM calls to complete a single task — multiplying token consumption proportionally. Agentic AI is the primary driver of the widening gap between falling per-token prices and rising enterprise AI bills.

Agentic AI ↑ Back to text

A category of artificial intelligence systems that act autonomously over extended periods to accomplish complex, multi-step goals. Agentic AI systems can plan, use tools, retrieve information, write and run code, and adapt their behavior based on intermediate results — functioning more like an autonomous worker than a question-and-answer interface. Goldman Sachs projects a 24-fold increase in token consumption by 2030 driven primarily by agentic adoption.

Tokenmaxxing

A colloquial term for the practice of maximizing AI token usage, often in response to corporate cultures that measure AI adoption as a performance metric. When companies create leaderboards or incentives based on AI usage rather than outcomes, employees may consume tokens aggressively and indiscriminately, generating large bills without proportional productivity gains. The term emerged in 2026 alongside reports that some individual users were generating monthly token bills exceeding $150,000.

AI Washing

The practice of attributing business decisions — particularly layoffs and restructurings — to artificial intelligence adoption when the underlying drivers are financial performance issues, geopolitical pressure, or routine cost-cutting. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman acknowledged the phenomenon publicly in 2026. Deutsche Bank analysts predicted it would be “a significant feature of 2026.” AI washing complicates the measurement of genuine AI-driven displacement by conflating strategic narrative with causal reality.

Workslop

A term coined by Stanford and BetterUp researchers to describe low-quality, low-effort AI-generated content that is passed along in professional workflows, creating downstream work for recipients who must correct, verify, or redo it. Workslop is a measurable source of productivity loss that partially offsets productivity gains from AI adoption. Studies estimate that each workslop incident costs recipients approximately two hours of remediation time.
