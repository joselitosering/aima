DATA CENTERS IN ORBIT: Why Big Tech Wants to Move AI's Power Problem to Space

KN
Kenji Nakamoto
Technology Writer & Explorer · AIMA
Published
July 7, 2026
Read Time
8 min read
Category
AI Science

Last month, a 130-pound satellite carrying an Nvidia H100 GPU launched into Low-Earth Orbit and started doing actual work—running AI inference for paying customers. Not a prototype. Not a publicity stunt. Real compute, in space, generating revenue. If you've been following the scramble over data center power, you know why this matters: artificial intelligence is thirsty, and Earth's grid is running dry. But what if we stopped trying to squeeze more power from the ground, and looked up instead?

The Power Problem

The numbers are brutal. In 2025, worldwide data centers consumed 104 gigawatts of power. In 2026, that number jumped to 132 gigawatts—a 27% increase in a single year. By 2030, we're looking at 290 gigawatts. That's not just growth; that's a trajectory.

AI is the accelerant. Specialized AI servers—the kind with tensor processing units designed for large language models—burned through 95 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2025. This year, that number reached 175 terawatt-hours. That's an 84% jump. To put it in perspective, 175 terawatt-hours is roughly the annual electricity consumption of Australia. And this is just the beginning.

The grid can't keep pace. Electric utilities are adding 2–3 gigawatts of new generating capacity per year. Data center operators need 5–7 gigawatts per year. We're in deficit, and the gap widens every quarter.

The Sun Never Sets in Space

In November 2025, Google Research published Project Suncatcher—a proposal so elegant it deserves explaining in plain language.

Earth-based solar panels get eight hours of useful sunlight per day, interrupted by clouds, seasons, and darkness. But in a specific sun-synchronous Low-Earth Orbit at roughly 650 kilometers altitude, a satellite stays perpetually at the boundary between day and night. The sun is always there, at the same angle, unobstructed. Google's modeling shows solar panels in that orbit can generate eight times more power per square meter than the best panels on Earth.

The design uses 81 satellites flying in a tight cluster, separated by 100–200 meters each, all linked by Free-Space Optical beams—essentially laser connections carrying data at 800 gigabits per second in both directions. Inside those satellites: Google's Trillium tensor processors, the chips powering Gemini. Solar power harvested from the vacuum of space, beamed directly into AI training.

Travis Beals, the lead researcher, was direct: "In the future, space may be the best place to scale AI compute." Sundar Pichai, Google's CEO, suggested that in roughly a decade, space-based data centers might feel as ordinary as the terrestrial kind.

In a sun-synchronous Low-Earth Orbit, the sun never sets. Solar panels generate eight times more power per square meter than on Earth.

The Thermal Reckoning

Here's where physics enters with a cold, hard reality.

A single Nvidia H100 GPU, running at full capacity, dissipates 700 watts of heat. In a data center on Earth, you pump cold water through cooling loops and dump waste heat into the atmosphere. In space, there's no atmosphere. There's no water circulation. The only way to shed heat is through Radiative Cooling—using Thermal Radiator surfaces that emit energy as infrared radiation. The Stefan-Boltzmann Law governs this relationship—a principle so unforgiving that radiator area must scale directly with power draw.

One H100 needs roughly three square meters of Thermal Radiator surface. A full 40-kilowatt server rack—32 GPUs stacked together—needs about 80 square meters of radiator. Picture a pickleball court of radiator panel, launched into space, kept perfectly aligned, shedding heat into vacuum. And that radiator degrades: Atomic Oxygen at orbital altitudes slowly eats away at the material's ability to radiate. After five years, you're losing roughly 40% of cooling efficiency to plain oxidation.

The physics doesn't immediately kill the idea. But it demands massive engineering overhead.

The Economics Aren't There Yet

Andrew McCalip, a thermal-systems engineer at Varda Space Industries, ran the numbers. His verdict: "The physics doesn't immediately kill it, but the economics are savage."

Orbital compute costs roughly three times as much per watt as a terrestrial data center, accounting for launch costs, radiation-hardened hardware, and the extra mass needed for survival and cooling. Google's own model concedes the whole thing only makes financial sense if launch costs fall from today's roughly $1,500 per kilogram down to under $200 per kilogram by the mid-2030s.

That's not a small bet. That's betting the entire business case on a sevenfold cost reduction that hasn't happened yet.

A Moonshot Worth Watching

Yet here's what fascinates me: people are building it anyway.

Starcloud, a Seattle startup, raised $170 million in Series A funding at a $1.1 billion valuation—the fastest any Y Combinator company has reached unicorn status. In November 2025, they launched Starcloud-1, a 130-pound satellite with an H100. They're launching Starcloud-2 later this year with an Nvidia Blackwell B200, carrying 100 times the power generation of Starcloud-1. Varda Space Industries is running production workloads. Blue Origin is filing for massive constellations. SpaceX is building, too.

None of this is capital-efficient today. But the trajectory is clear: launch costs are falling, satellite design is improving, and the desperate need for power keeps rising. At some point—maybe five years, maybe fifteen—the graphs will cross. When they do, the next generation of AI training might happen 400 kilometers above your head, powered by sunlight that never stops shining.

That's worth paying attention to.

Glossary

Low-Earth Orbit ↑ Back to text

A stable orbital zone at altitudes of roughly 200–2,000 kilometers above Earth's surface, where satellites experience near-continuous sunlight and can maintain operational orbits for years without constant station-keeping.

Free-Space Optical ↑ Back to text

Wireless communication using laser beams to transmit data through the vacuum of space, achieving extremely high data rates (in this case, 800+ gigabits per second per link) without the signal loss or latency of terrestrial networks.

Radiative Cooling ↑ Back to text

The process of shedding heat energy by emitting thermal radiation directly into space, the only cooling mechanism available in the vacuum of orbit where air-based convection and water-loop systems are impossible.

Thermal Radiator ↑ Back to text

A specially designed surface panel aboard a spacecraft that absorbs waste heat from electronics and re-emits it as infrared radiation into space; surface area must scale directly with the amount of heat that needs to be dissipated.

Stefan-Boltzmann Law ↑ Back to text

A fundamental physics principle stating that the radiant heat energy emitted by a surface is proportional to the fourth power of its absolute temperature, constraining radiator efficiency as target temperatures rise.

Atomic Oxygen ↑ Back to text

Highly reactive oxygen atoms found in the upper atmosphere at Low-Earth Orbit altitudes, which gradually oxidize and degrade spacecraft radiator materials over time, reducing their heat-rejection efficiency by up to 40% over a five-year mission.
