THE GOVERNMENT FILED A BRIEF FOR THE ALGORITHM: How the DOJ Killed America's First AI Antidiscrimination Law

DG
Dawn Ginhaua
Cultural Critic & Educator · AIMA
Published
July 7, 2026
Read Time
8 min read
Category
AI Ethics

Mary Louis paid her rent on time for sixteen years. She held a Section 8 voucher, a clean record, and an algorithm's verdict against her anyway. SafeRent Solutions' screening software didn't know she was Black. It only knew she had a voucher, and it treated that fact as a debt. She was denied housing. She sued, and won nearly $2.3 million (Leadership Conference on Civil and Human Rights). Colorado tried to write a law so the next Mary Louis wouldn't need a lawsuit. The United States Department of Justice filed a brief to stop it.

The Law They Filed a Brief to Kill

Colorado's SB24-205, signed May 17, 2024, was America's first comprehensive AI antidiscrimination law. It did not ban AI. It did not dictate how a model should be built. It required developers and deployers of "high-risk" systems — the ones making consequential calls in hiring, lending, housing, healthcare, and government services — to exercise "reasonable care" to prevent algorithmic discrimination (Leadership Conference on Civil and Human Rights). That is the same standard applied to every other product capable of hurting people. It took effect nowhere. Enforcement, originally set for February 2026, was pushed to June 30 — and then suspended entirely before it ever began.

Corporate Litigation, Federal Co-Counsel

On April 9, 2026, Elon Musk's xAI sued Colorado's attorney general. Fifteen days later, the DOJ intervened — the first time the federal government has moved to invalidate a state AI law in court, under authority certified by Assistant Attorney General Harmeet Dhillon as being "of general public importance" (Norton Rose Fulbright; National Law Review). Dhillon's public statement: laws requiring AI companies to prevent discrimination "infect their products with woke DEI ideology" and amount to states trying "to coerce technological innovators into producing harmful products" (U.S. Department of Justice). Three days after intervention, enforcement was suspended. This was not government moving at government speed. Executive Order 14365, signed December 11, 2025, had already named Colorado's law a target and ordered a DOJ task force built for exactly this fight, established January 9, 2026 (White House). The lawsuit was the plan's execution, not its origin.

The Constitutional Argument, Turned Inside Out

Here is the maneuver, stated plainly. The DOJ invoked the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause — the clause behind Brown v. Board of Education — to argue that requiring companies to prevent discrimination is itself a form of discrimination. Colorado's law exempted AI systems designed to "increase diversity or redress historical discrimination." The DOJ argued that exemption was an unconstitutional racial classification, citing Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard (2023) as precedent (National Law Review). Preventing bias, in this framing, requires "demographic-conscious engineering," and demographic-conscious engineering is discrimination. The law built to stop harm was recast as the harm.

The new law tells you there is an algorithm. The old law required the algorithm not to discriminate against you. That is the difference between a warning label and a seat belt.

The Harm That Needed No Law to Document

One month after enforcement was suspended, Stanford HAI published the largest audit of AI hiring tools ever conducted — 3.4 million job seekers, 150 employers, one dominant vendor. Twenty-six percent of Black applicants and 15% of Asian applicants faced positions where the tool discriminated against their group; roughly 40,000 additional Black and Asian applications would have advanced under equal treatment (Stanford HAI). The harm was never hypothetical. It was measured the same month the law meant to address it went dark.

What the Replacement Law Does

Under legal duress, Colorado's legislature rewrote the statute. SB 26-189, signed May 14, 2026, passed 57-6 and 34-1 — bipartisan majorities voting to strip the law's core obligation. Gone: the duty of reasonable care, mandatory risk management programs, mandatory impact assessments, and the free-standing cause of action for algorithmic discrimination (Crowell & Moring). What remains is disclosure — a notice that an algorithm was involved, a window to contest an outcome after the fact. Nothing in the new law requires the algorithm to be less discriminatory. It requires only that you be told, later, that it might have been.

Rep. Brianna Titone, the original bill's lead sponsor, called the DOJ's constitutional claims "a distraction" (Colorado Sun). She may be right that the argument was a mechanism rather than the point. The point is the outcome: no AI company operating in America is legally obligated to prevent its systems from discriminating against the people those systems screen, price, and judge. The Department of Justice that once enforced the Civil Rights Act now cites it to disarm a civil rights statute. The Equal Protection Clause, written to shield people from the state, has been redeployed to shield a model from the people. If the DOJ is xAI's co-counsel and the Fourteenth Amendment is the algorithm's lawyer, who exactly represents the next Mary Louis?

Glossary

Disparate Impact ↑ Back to text

A legal doctrine holding that a policy or system can be unlawfully discriminatory based on its unequal outcomes for a protected group, regardless of intent.

Reasonable Care Standard ↑ Back to text

SB24-205's core obligation, requiring AI developers and deployers to take ordinary precautions against foreseeable discriminatory harm — the same liability threshold applied to most consumer products.

Equal Protection Clause ↑ Back to text

The Fourteenth Amendment provision barring states from denying any person equal protection of the law; historically the constitutional basis for civil rights enforcement.

High-Risk AI System ↑ Back to text

Under Colorado's original law, an AI system making or substantially influencing consequential decisions in sectors like employment, lending, housing, or healthcare.

Demographic-Conscious Engineering ↑ Back to text

The DOJ's term for adjusting an algorithm's design to correct for racial or gender disparities in its outcomes — the practice its complaint argued was itself unconstitutional.

AI Litigation Task Force ↑ Back to text

A DOJ unit established January 9, 2026 under Executive Order 14365 with the sole mandate of challenging state AI laws in court.

Automated Decision-Making Technology (ADMT) ↑ Back to text

The narrower category used in SB 26-189, covering systems that process personal data to materially influence a consequential decision — the replacement framework's scope term.
