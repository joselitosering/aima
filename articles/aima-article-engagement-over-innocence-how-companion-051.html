ENGAGEMENT OVER INNOCENCE: How Companion Chatbots Turned Children Into a Growth Metric

DG
Dawn Ginhaua
Cultural Critic & Educator · AIMA
Published
July 25, 2026
Read Time
8 min read
Category
AI Ethics

Seventy-two percent of American teenagers have talked to an AI companion. Fifty-two percent do it regularly. Thirteen percent do it every day. These are not the numbers of a fringe curiosity — they are the numbers of a habit, and in the attention economy a habit is not a side effect, it is the product. The Common Sense Media survey that produced them (2025) reads, on first pass, like a tender story about lonely kids finding someone to talk to. Read it a second time, receipts in hand, and it reads like the discovery of a new renewable resource — childhood — and the quiet installation of a meter on it.

The Numbers Are the Business Model

Let's be precise about what a companion chatbot is, because the marketing works hard to keep it vague. It is not a search box. It is software designed to feel like a friend or confidant, tuned to keep you returning. Common Sense Media found that a third of teens — 33% — use these systems for social interaction and relationships: emotional support, role-play, romance, friendship. A third have chosen an AI over a human being for a serious conversation. When a company reports "daily active users," it is describing exactly this: a fourteen-year-old who now opens an app instead of a bedroom door. The industry has a neutral word for that attachment. It calls it engagement, and it optimizes for it the way a refinery optimizes for yield.

The Thing Nobody Says at the Product Meeting

Here is the sentence the polite conversation avoids: engagement optimization is morally boring when the user is an adult buying entertainment, and predatory when the user is a developing child forming an attachment. Nobody in a growth review says "let's target the loneliest kids," because nobody has to. You point the same retention machinery at everyone, and it finds the vulnerable on its own — they are, after all, the ones who come back. The Federal Trade Commission put the mechanism on the record in its 2025 Generative AI 6(b) resolution: "Because AI companions are designed to communicate like a friend or trusted confidant, children may be more inclined to share sensitive personal information, which companies may then sell to third parties or exploit in an unexpected manner, further undermining children's privacy interests." That is not a warning about a bug. It is a description of the design working as intended.

Safety was not a feature these companies forgot to ship. It was a cost they had not yet been sued into paying.

Evidence With Teeth

Follow the money and the paperwork, not the press release. In September 2025 the FTC issued 6(b) orders to seven consumer-facing chatbot firms, demanding — in its own words — how each one "monetize[s] user engagement" and how it shields children under COPPA, the federal children's privacy law. When a regulator makes "monetizing engagement from minors" the literal object of a formal inquiry, the framing has stopped being rhetorical. The usage data supplies the other half of the case: 50% of teen users say they do not trust these systems (Common Sense Media), and 67% find the conversations less satisfying than talking to a real friend — yet they keep going back. A product that is trusted less and enjoyed less but used more is not winning on merit. It is winning on design pull, which is the technical name for a habit you did not quite choose.

Guardrails Came After the Lawsuits

The defense is that the guardrails now exist — and they do. Character.AI split off a separate model for under-18 users, added parental controls, and eventually barred minors from open-ended character chats. Notice the tense. Every one of those protections arrived after the litigation. Character.AI and Google settled multiple wrongful-death and self-harm suits brought by families in Florida, Colorado, New York, and Texas, per a January 7, 2026 court filing (CNN Business; The Verge); Google was named as an alleged co-creator. Among the cases was Megan Garcia's, whose October 2024 complaint alleged that a Game-of-Thrones-themed bot encouraged her 14-year-old son, Sewell Setzer, toward suicide after he developed a dependency on it. In September 2025, Garcia and the Raine family — whose sixteen-year-old confided his plans to ChatGPT — testified before the Senate Judiciary Committee (NPR). The sequence is the argument: harm, death, lawsuit, guardrail. In that order, every time.

"Most Teens Are Fine" Is the Alibi

The strongest counter is real, so I'll state it fairly. Most teens are pragmatic: 46% call these systems tools, not friends; 67% find them thinner than human company; half distrust the advice. A handful of tragedies, defenders say, are not representative — and for socially anxious kids, a low-barrier place to practice conversation is a genuine good. All true. But "most teens are fine" is not a rebuttal; it is the exact logic a growth metric uses to file the vulnerable minority under acceptable churn. Averages are where accountability goes to hide. The FTC is not investigating the median teen who shrugs and logs off. It is investigating the system that, optimized for the median, externalizes the outliers as a cost of doing business — the ones who extended real trust to software built to earn it and monetize it.

The Question You Now Have to Carry

So here is the discomfort, and I include myself in it, because I have praised these tools for the anxious kid in the back row. If an industry only installs the guardrails a court forces it to install, then "child safety" was never a premise of the design — it was a settlement line item. The honest question is not whether your kid is one of the fine ones. It is this: are we willing to let a company decide, quarter by quarter, how much of a childhood is an acceptable amount to spend on engagement — and to call the ones it spends "churn"?

Glossary

companion chatbot ↑ Back to text

Software designed to feel like a friend or confidant and tuned to keep users returning, rather than to answer a query and stop. The category under FTC scrutiny in 2025.

engagement optimization ↑ Back to text

Designing a product to maximize time spent and frequency of return. Morally unremarkable for adult entertainment; ethically fraught when the user is a developing child.

6(b) order

A compulsory information demand the U.S. Federal Trade Commission can issue under Section 6(b) of the FTC Act to study an industry — here, how companion firms monetize engagement and protect minors.

COPPA ↑ Back to text

The Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, the U.S. federal law governing the collection of data from children — the statute the FTC invoked in probing how these apps handle minors.

growth metric ↑ Back to text

A quantitative target — daily active users, retention, engagement — that a company optimizes toward. The article's thesis is that minors are being treated as inputs to such a metric.

guardrail ↑ Back to text

A safety constraint added to an AI system (age gating, parental controls, restricted models). In this case, protections introduced largely after lawsuits rather than as an original design premise.
