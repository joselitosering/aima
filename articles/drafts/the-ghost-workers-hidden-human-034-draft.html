The machine that wrote your last memo, summarized your last meeting, and generated a dozen marketing hooks before your coffee cooled was taught by someone earning less than $2 an hour. That is not a metaphor about automation displacing workers somewhere in the abstract future. That is a payroll record from a BPO contractor in Nairobi, Kenya, commissioned by OpenAI, investigated and published by TIME magazine in January 2023. The "autonomy" of modern AI is a carefully constructed illusion — assembled layer by layer, outsourced contract by outsourced contract — and the people who built it are paying for that illusion with their health, their stability, and in many cases, their dignity.

The $2 Teacher Behind the $200 Billion Machine

In early 2023, TIME investigative reporter Billy Perrigo obtained documents showing that Kenyan workers employed by Sama — a firm contracted by OpenAI to build ChatGPT's content safety filter — were earning between $1.32 and $2 per hour depending on seniority and performance. OpenAI was paying Sama approximately $12.50 per hour per worker (Perrigo). The gap between what the AI company paid and what the labeler received is not a rounding error. It is the profit model, and it is repeated at scale across the global data annotation supply chain.

These workers were performing RLHF — reinforcement learning from human feedback — the process by which large language models are refined using human judgment about quality and safety. Which response is more helpful? Which answer is less harmful? Every preference label, every correction that shapes how a chatbot learns to sound reasonable and trustworthy, was produced by a human being sitting at a screen. The technology that appears to think for itself was, at every critical juncture, corrected by human hands.

What those hands were correcting, in many cases, was some of the worst content the internet produces — violent imagery, sexual abuse material, graphic extremism — reviewed at volume with minimal psychological support. The Kenyan workers later described their conditions in a collective statement as coming "at great cost to our health, our lives and our families… for less than $2 per hour" (Kenyan AI Data Labelers).

Ghost Work: A Design Choice, Not a Side Effect

Microsoft researchers Mary L. Gray and Siddharth Suri named this phenomenon in their 2019 book: ghost work is the invisible human labor that powers apps, websites, and AI systems — workers hired to do tasks that are made to look automated. The invisibility is not incidental. It is structural. Platforms are designed so workers cannot see each other, cannot see the final product their labor feeds into, and cannot build collective power. Gray and Suri estimated that roughly 8% of Americans had participated in this hidden economy even before the AI boom accelerated demand for annotation and content moderation labor (Gray and Suri).

Ghost work is a design choice because every technical decision that structures these platforms — no fixed shifts, no persistent worker identities, no ability to communicate across task batches — has the downstream effect of preventing organizing and shared identity. That outcome is too consistent to be accidental. The labor is priced as disposable because the architecture makes it behave as disposable. What appears to be a natural feature of the digital economy is, in practice, a governance decision about who deserves to be seen.

The Supply Chain of Invisibility

The data annotation industry is not a cottage trade. Market research aggregators place the global annotation tools market at approximately $2.37 billion in 2025, growing at roughly 32% annually — a rate that quantifies how much economic value rides on this hidden labor layer (Grand View Research). The World Bank estimates that between 154 million and 435 million people globally are engaged in online gig work — roughly 4 to 12 percent of the world's labor force — and AI data work draws from this pool at scale (World Bank).

The supply chain runs like this: an AI company like OpenAI or Meta needs training data. They contract a BPO — a business process outsourcing firm like Sama, Scale AI, or Surge AI. The BPO recruits annotators through platforms like Remotasks or Mechanical Turk. Workers in Kenya, the Philippines, India, or Venezuela complete tasks in short batches, often without knowing who the end client is. Each intermediary layer adds margin and removes accountability. By the time wages are set, the people doing the work are four or five contracts removed from the company whose valuation their labor underwrites. Oxford Economics estimated that the U.S. data annotation industry alone supported nearly 200,000 flexible earning opportunities and approximately 9,000 full-time-equivalent jobs in 2024 — the domestic slice of a far larger global workforce whose wages tell a different story (Oxford Economics).

The Flexibility Trap

Ghost work is not a ladder. It is a conveyor belt designed to run until you fall off.

The industry's defenders make a real argument: in regions with high unemployment, a remote AI labeling job that pays $2 an hour may genuinely exceed local median wages and offer income not otherwise available. "Earning opportunities" — the phrase Oxford Economics uses — are real. The question is not whether these jobs exist. The question is whether they are dignified, stable, or lead anywhere.

The evidence on stability is damning. In India, monthly wages for data annotators reportedly sit around ₹15,000 to ₹20,000 — roughly $180 to $240 — and appear to have remained roughly stagnant since 2019 despite the industry's explosive growth (Qhala). In practice, "flexibility" means unpaid training periods, accuracy quotas calibrated to produce failure, and abrupt mass lockouts without notice or severance. In March 2024, Scale AI's Remotasks platform deactivated workers in Kenya, Nigeria, and Pakistan simultaneously — no warning, no explanation, no recourse. Workers who had structured their household income around this precarious labor arrangement simply found their accounts closed overnight.

The psychological toll compounds the economic injury. Content moderation — reviewing graphic violence, exploitation material, and extremist content before it enters AI training datasets or reaches consumer platforms — requires sustained exposure to trauma at volume. The standard contractor agreement notes that workers may encounter disturbing material. It rarely includes meaningful therapeutic support. The contract is designed to insulate the platform from liability; the worker absorbs the damage.

The Illusion Is Starting to Crack

Something shifted in 2025. Surge AI faced a California class-action lawsuit alleging the company misclassified annotators as independent contractors and structured training requirements and deadlines to extract unpaid labor (TechEquity Collaborative). TechEquity Collaborative published "Ghost Workers in the Machine," featuring U.S.-based data workers speaking out about exploitation by Big Tech — evidence that this is no longer only an offshore story told about distant places we can afford not to look at. CBS News 60 Minutes aired an investigation into Kenyan AI labelers describing overwork, underpayment, and psychological harm caused by American technology companies (CBS News). Workers are finding each other across platforms deliberately designed to keep them isolated. They are building a record. The architecture of invisibility is under pressure in a way it has never been in a decade of exponential AI growth.

Each lawsuit, each published testimony, each broadcast investigation makes it fractionally harder to sell the story that AI is autonomous — that it emerged from mathematics rather than from a global supply chain of undervalued human judgment. The question now is whether the companies that profit from AI, and the creators who build on top of it, will respond before those conditions calcify into something far harder to change.

What We Owe the Hands That Taught the Machine

I have built content pipelines that run on these models. I use language model outputs to accelerate editorial work that once required teams and weeks. I believe in the tools. But believing in the tools does not require pretending they appeared from mathematics alone. Every response that feels intelligent, every safety guardrail that deflects harm, every fine-tuned preference that makes a chatbot useful rather than chaotic — all of it passed through human hands first. Those hands deserve to be named, counted, and compensated at a rate that reflects the value they created, not the value their invisibility permitted the industry to extract.

The first concrete step is the one that changed labor conditions in garment manufacturing and consumer electronics: supply chain transparency. Ask your AI vendors where their training data came from. Ask whether the annotation workforce was directly employed, contracted through a BPO, or crowdsourced through a gig platform with no binding labor protections. Ask what the workers were paid. If your vendor cannot answer, that absence is itself an answer. The AI economy will generate real wealth — generational wealth, transformative wealth. The only question that matters is who gets to keep it.

Ghost Work
A term coined by researchers Mary L. Gray and Siddharth Suri for invisible human labor that powers apps, websites, and AI systems — tasks designed to appear automated but performed by real workers hired without stable contracts, shared identity, or collective power. Ghost work is a structural design outcome, not an accident of the gig economy.
RLHF
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback — the training process by which large language models are refined using human ratings and preference signals. Human workers rate AI outputs for quality, accuracy, and safety; their judgments shape how the model behaves. RLHF is the central mechanism that makes AI systems feel intelligent and the primary driver of global demand for low-wage annotation labor at scale.
Data Annotation
The process of labeling, tagging, and classifying raw data — text, images, video, or audio — so that machine learning systems can use it for training. Data annotation is the foundational labor layer of nearly all modern AI systems and the site of the most concentrated wage exploitation in the AI supply chain, with the market growing at an estimated 32% annually through the mid-2030s.
Content Moderation
The review and removal of harmful, illegal, or policy-violating material from digital platforms and AI training datasets. Content moderation workers are regularly exposed to graphic violence, exploitation material, and extremist content at volume; the work carries significant psychological risk and is disproportionately outsourced to low-wage contractors — often in the Global South — with inadequate mental health support.
BPO
Business Process Outsourcing — the practice of contracting operational tasks to third-party firms, typically offshore. In the AI industry, BPO firms like Sama, Scale AI, and Surge AI serve as intermediaries between AI companies and annotation workforces, adding a margin layer that reduces costs for the AI client while distancing it from direct employment obligations and accountability toward individual workers.
Precarious Labor
Work characterized by short-term or no contracts, variable and unpredictable income, absence of benefits and employment protections, and high disposability from the platform's perspective. Data annotation and content moderation work in the AI supply chain operates almost entirely within precarious labor structures — annotators are classified as independent contractors regardless of how consistently or exclusively they work for a single platform.
