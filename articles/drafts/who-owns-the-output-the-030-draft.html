Last week a federal judge approved a $1.5 billion settlement in Bartz v. Anthropic — the largest copyright payout in the short, turbulent history of generative AI. I have been building with these tools for two years. I have used them to write, score, edit, and automate things that used to cost me weeks and contract labor. And when I read that number, my first thought wasn't triumph or schadenfreude. It was: we are all operating on borrowed legal time, and most of us don't know it yet.

The Question That Started a $1.5 Billion Argument

The authors who sued Anthropic weren't arguing about creativity. They were arguing about property. Specifically: did Anthropic have the right to train its Claude models on books those authors wrote — books never licensed for machine consumption, never cleared for training data? The settlement approved on July 20, 2026 didn't fully answer that. What it did was draw a sharper line than anyone had before.

The court issued what legal analysts are calling a split fair use ruling. Training an AI model on lawfully acquired copyrighted books can qualify as fair use — that part of Anthropic's defense held. But storing pirated copies of those books in a centralized library does not qualify, full stop. The $1.5 billion liability wasn't about training as a concept. It was about how Anthropic acquired the training data in the first place (Norton Rose Fulbright). That distinction matters enormously, and it's the one the rest of the industry is now scrambling to understand.

The pending cases against OpenAI, Microsoft, Meta, and Getty/Stability are still working through the broader question. The Bartz settlement is a milestone, not a finish line. The most consequential legal fight in the history of creative work is still being decided, one courtroom at a time.

What a Prompt Cannot Do

While the lawsuits pile up on one side of the legal system, the U.S. Copyright Office has been quietly drawing its own lines on the other. In January 2025, the Office published its Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability report. On the central question of copyrightability, its position is unambiguous: works generated entirely by AI are not eligible for copyright protection.

The language the Office used is worth sitting with carefully: "Given current generally available technology, prompts alone do not provide sufficient human control to make users of an AI system the authors of the output." That one sentence has consequences for anyone building a content business on generative AI — including me.

"Prompts alone do not provide sufficient human control to make users of an AI system the authors of the output." — U.S. Copyright Office, Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability (January 2025)

What is protectable is the human-authored contribution: the selection, arrangement, modification, and creative direction a person applies to AI-generated material. The Copyright Office has granted registrations for hundreds of mixed human/AI works since its 2023 guidance — protection attaching to what the person did, not what the model produced. The implication for practitioners is practical, not philosophical: document your creative decisions, because those decisions are where your rights live. The AI output is the raw material. What you do with it is the work.

A Litigation Tidal Wave with No Shoreline

One leading tracker, J.S. Held's AI Disputes Monitor, recorded 42 new AI-related lawsuits filed in the second quarter of 2026 alone — a 35% increase over Q1. Their total tracked dataset reached 426 cases, with copyright claims dominating the docket. Year-to-date filings by mid-2026 had already hit roughly 86% of the entire 2025 total. The pace is not slowing. Different trackers count differently depending on scope and jurisdiction, but the directional signal is uniform: this is accelerating.

James E. Malackowski, Chief Intellectual Property Officer at J.S. Held, noted that "the economic questions are becoming more robust, and courts will need well supported answers" — specifically around licensing benchmarks and IP cost analysis as theories of harm grow more refined. Real-time competitive substitution. Structured-database scraping. Market harm to existing licensing revenue. These are not abstract claims anymore. They are the arguments that will determine how generative AI gets used and priced for the next decade.

The counterargument from model builders is not without force. Defenders argue that transformative use — training on copyrighted works to learn statistical patterns, not to republish them — is exactly what fair use was designed to protect. Requiring per-work licensing clearance for every item in a training corpus, they say, would make open research economically impossible and entrench only the largest incumbents who can afford blanket licensing deals. That argument won on the training question in Bartz. It hasn't won everywhere. The cases still pending involve output-substitution claims that are harder to characterize as purely transformative.

The Creator's Real Exposure

Here is where I talk about this from where I actually sit. I am not a model builder. I don't train anything. I use generative AI as a production instrument — to write first drafts, generate image concepts, automate publishing pipelines that a five-person shop couldn't otherwise sustain. My exposure is not the training-data exposure. It is the output exposure.

If the content I publish is substantially AI-generated without meaningful human authorship layered on top, I have no copyright in it. Anyone can reproduce it freely. I cannot license it. I cannot build a catalogue worth protecting. The creative decisions I make around, through, and after the AI output are the only legally defensible thing I own. That is a fact I think about every single day.

That is not a reason to stop using these tools. It is a reason to use them more deliberately. The photographer doesn't own the light; they own the frame. The film editor doesn't own the footage; they own the cut. Generative AI belongs to the same category of instrument. The creative direction, the curation, the refinement, the judgment about what to keep and what to discard — that is where the work lives. That is where the rights live too.

Why the Settlement Changes the Economics

The Bartz settlement does something legal arguments alone never could: it puts a real number on what copyright exposure costs. $1.5 billion is not a warning. It is a data point that will reshape how model builders approach training data acquisition, how investors price AI companies, and — critically — how licensing markets for creative works develop over the next five years.

This is where the practitioner's interest diverges from the policy debate. Creators who have spent years building libraries of original work — writing, illustration, music, voice — now have a legitimate basis to negotiate licensing deals with AI companies that want access to high-quality, legally cleared training data. The litigation is forcing those negotiations to exist. For independent creators who have always been price-takers in a platform economy, the emergence of a real licensing market for creative work could be the most consequential economic shift in a generation. The lawsuits nobody wanted may produce the deals everybody needs.

The Practitioner's Obligation

I will keep using these tools. I will keep building pipelines that let one person do what five people used to do, and I believe that capacity — freed from its gatekeepers — is one of the most democratizing forces in the history of creative production. But I am not going to pretend the legal landscape is clear when it isn't, and I'm not going to pretend that clarity doesn't matter.

The Copyright Office's guidance on copyrightability is a starting point, not a finish line. The training-data cases will shape what models can learn from. The output-substitution cases will shape what creators can own. The licensing markets that emerge from both will shape who gets paid. Every creator building on generative AI is working inside that three-part uncertainty right now, whether they know it or not. Knowing it is better.

The first step — and I mean this practically — is to document your creative decisions. Keep records of what you directed, what you modified, what you selected, and why. That documentation is the evidence of human authorship that makes your output registerable and defensible. The law is still being written. Write yourself into it before someone else writes you out.

Fair Use
A legal doctrine under U.S. copyright law permitting limited use of copyrighted material without authorization, evaluated across four factors: the purpose of use, the nature of the original work, the amount used, and the effect on the market. The split ruling in Bartz v. Anthropic confirmed AI training on lawfully acquired works can qualify, but acquiring pirated source material does not.
Training Data
The corpus of text, images, audio, or other content used to build and fine-tune an AI model. The composition and sourcing of training data is the central dispute in every major generative AI copyright lawsuit currently pending against major model builders.
Generative AI
AI systems — including large language models and image generators — that produce new content by learning statistical patterns from large datasets. Distinct from AI that classifies or retrieves existing content; generative AI creates outputs that can directly compete with human-authored work in the marketplace, which is the root of the current IP crisis.
Copyrightability
The legal threshold a creative work must meet to qualify for copyright protection under U.S. law. The U.S. Copyright Office's January 2025 report established that copyrightability requires demonstrable human authorship; AI-only outputs do not meet this threshold, though human-authored elements within a mixed human/AI work remain individually protectable.
Transformative Use
A fair use category in which a new work adds something new — a different purpose, character, or meaning — rather than simply reproducing the original. The AI industry's core defense for training on copyrighted works is that learning statistical patterns constitutes transformative use rather than expressive reproduction of any specific work.
Licensing
A formal legal arrangement granting permission to use copyrighted material, typically in exchange for compensation. As AI copyright litigation accelerates and settlement costs become concrete, licensing markets for creative training data are emerging — offering creators who hold original content libraries a potential new and significant revenue stream.
