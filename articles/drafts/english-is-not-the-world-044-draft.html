There are about 350,000 people who speak Icelandic, and an artificial intelligence can hold a real conversation in it. There are 68 million people who speak Javanese, and it mostly cannot. Sit with that for a second. A language with roughly two hundred times fewer speakers is better served by the most powerful tools we have ever built. This is not an accident of difficulty. It is a decision — made by data, by markets, by the quiet assumption that English is the world. It is not. English is roughly a fifth of it, and the other four-fifths are being taught, one confident wrong answer at a time, that their words don't quite count.

The Language a Machine Wouldn't Learn

I found this out the way I find out most things: by trying it. I have watched an AI agent write flawless English marketing copy in ninety seconds, and then, asked to do the same work in the Filipino I grew up hearing at my grandmother's table, produce something fluent enough to fool me and wrong enough to embarrass me. The grammar was clean. The idioms were plausible. The facts underneath were quietly, cheerfully false. That gap has a shape, and the shape is global. English is spoken by about 19% of humanity — roughly 1.5 billion people, only 390 million of them native — yet it accounts for 45% of the world's web pages, 56% of the open datasets on Hugging Face, and nearly 98% of scientific papers (World Bank, Digital Progress and Trends Report, 2025). We didn't build a mirror of the world. We built a mirror of the internet, and the internet has an accent.

How a Monoculture Gets Built

A language monoculture is not a conspiracy. It is a feedback loop, and feedback loops are patient. A model trained mostly on English performs best in English. That poor performance in other languages, the researchers Saurabh Khanna and Xinxu Li write, "discourages speakers from using their languages digitally, reducing digital content generation, which further reduces training data, which worsens performance — self-reinforcing cycles where invisibility begets further invisibility" (Invisible Languages of the LLM Universe, 2025). Their map of the world's 7,613 living languages is bracing. Around 27% — the ones they call Invisible Giants — have millions of speakers and almost no digital footprint, effectively absent from the data these systems learn from. Only 33 languages, four-tenths of one percent, reach full digital capability. Everything else is fading, echoing, or already gone from the machine's memory. As Khanna and Li put it, English dominance "is not a technical necessity but an artifact of power structures that systematically exclude marginalized linguistic knowledge."

The Fluency Illusion

Here is the part that should worry you most, because it is the part designed to be invisible. When researchers benchmarked eight leading models across 2,000 responses, accuracy fell from 90.7% in English to 76.9% in Kazakh and 74.0% in Mongolian (Beibitkhan & Abdul-Salem, 2026). But fluency barely moved. The sentences stayed smooth. This is the fluency illusion: the model sounds every bit as authoritative in a low-resource language as it does in English, while being far more likely to be wrong. A broken tool that stutters is honest — you know not to trust it. A tool that speaks your language beautifully and lies is something else. It is a confidence machine pointed at exactly the people least able to fact-check it against the English source it secretly trusts more. For six billion people, the danger was never that AI would refuse to speak. It's that it would speak fluently, and be wrong, and never let on.

What Collapses, and What Doesn't

Now the hopeful, unromantic accounting. Some of this is a solvable engineering problem. Cross-lingual transfer — letting a model reason in English and translate its answer out — helps a little. Multilingual-specific models exist. And the imbalance eases outside of text: only about 21% of YouTube video is English, versus 45% of web pages (World Bank, 2025), which means the raw human material for more diverse AI already exists, in voices and faces rather than URLs. But honesty requires the caveats. Cross-lingual transfer delivered gains of only 0.4 to 4.3 points in that Kazakh and Mongolian study, and the one model explicitly built for broad multilingual coverage performed worst on the very languages it claimed to serve, collapsing to 15.7% in Kazakh. The tools are real. The gap is not closing on its own. What collapses cheaply is the cost of trying; what doesn't collapse is the work of building good data where none exists.

Whose Knowledge Counts

This is where I stop being a technologist and start being the grandson of people whose language was never going to be in a dataset. Linguists warn of digital language death — the projection that only a small fraction of human languages will survive with real digital vitality. AI is not a neutral witness to that extinction. It is an accelerant, because it decides, invisibly and at scale, whose knowledge is retrievable and whose is noise. Khanna and Li call this "digital-epistemic injustice," and the phrase is exact: it is not only about words but about what a civilization is allowed to remember. When a Javanese farmer, a Quechua nurse, a Cebuano teacher asks a machine a question and gets fluent nonsense, the message underneath is old and colonial and wrong — that real knowledge lives elsewhere, in another tongue. A tool is supposed to lower the cost of making something that matters. This one, left as it is, raises the cost of mattering at all.

What We Build Instead

So here is the concrete first step, because a conclusion that doesn't point somewhere real is just a sigh. If you build with AI in a community whose language is under-served, start contributing to the data instead of only consuming it. Record and transcribe. Translate what your family already knows into text a model can learn from. Support the open, native-language corpora and community benchmarks — like MuBench and the African-language projects cited below — that measure the gap instead of hiding it. Every transcript you make is a vote against invisibility, a small deposit in a language's digital future. I am building toward a foundation, and this is squarely part of what I mean by legacy: not accumulation, but what you leave behind that keeps giving. English is not the world. The next generation of these tools will only know that if we teach it — one recorded voice, one honest dataset, one refusal to accept fluent silence at a time. Start with your own words. They were always worth training on.
