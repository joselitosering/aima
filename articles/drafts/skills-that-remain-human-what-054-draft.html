I have spent two years handing pieces of my work to machines. Research, first drafts, rough cuts of music videos I once needed a team and a month to finish — AI agents do that now, and it has been the most freeing thing I have done as a creator. So it surprised me, this year, to find myself obsessed with the opposite question. Not what AI can do for me, but what it cannot do at all — and why that boundary might be the most valuable thing a working person owns right now.

The honest answer turned out to be documented, measured, and stranger than the hype on either side. The machine, it turns out, has already drawn the line itself. Our job is to decide which side of it we invest in before the water rises.

The Machine Drew Its Own Boundary

In 2025, the World Economic Forum ran an experiment that should be more famous than it is. Working with the labor-analytics team at Indeed, they let GPT-4o evaluate its own capacity to substitute for humans across more than 2,800 granular workplace skills. The model scored itself. And across all 2,800-plus skills, it rated exactly zero of them as having "very high capacity" to be replaced by current generative AI. Sixty-nine percent it rated "very low" or "low." The skills it flagged as having no substitution potential at all were the human ones: empathy, active listening, sensory processing, and manual dexterity (World Economic Forum, 2025).

The report's own language is worth sitting with. "Skills rooted in human interaction — including empathy and active listening, and sensory processing abilities — and manual dexterity, endurance and precision, currently show no substitution potential due to their physical and deeply human components," the WEF wrote. "These findings underscore the practical limitations of current GenAI models, which lack the physicality to perform tasks that require hands-on interaction" (World Economic Forum, 2025).

I want to be careful here, because a machine grading itself is not a law of physics. But the pattern it drew is corroborated everywhere you look, and it points to something real.

A Rising Tide, Not a Crashing Wave

The most rigorous recent look at automation comes from a working paper out of MIT — Mertens, Thompson, and colleagues — built on more than 60,000 evaluations by experienced workers across 6,000-plus text-based tasks (Mertens and Thompson, 2026). Their finding reframes the whole conversation. AI models already complete roughly 60% of text-based tasks at minimally sufficient quality. Frontier models went from 60% to 70% success on 90-minute tasks in a single year. If the trend holds, they project AI could handle most text-based work at 88% to 97% success by 2030.

Their metaphor is the important part. Automation is not a "crashing wave" — one job category obliterated overnight. It is a "rising tide," advancing steadily and broadly across everything that can be typed. As the authors put it, "AI is unlikely to suddenly outperform humans across large groups of tasks from one year to the next," but "a rising tide implies that AI capabilities will improve across a wide range of text-based tasks, leaving fewer areas of work fully insulated from automation pressure" (Mertens and Thompson, 2026). That 88-to-97% figure is an extrapolation, not an observation — a projection from a paper still awaiting peer review. But even discounted, the direction is unmistakable. The water is coming up. The only question that matters is what stands above it.

Three Bottlenecks the Water Hasn't Reached

When you gather the frameworks that predict which work survives — and their lineage runs back through peer-reviewed research by Frey and Osborne and by Autor, Levy, and Murnane — they converge on three durable automation bottlenecks (Steele, 2024). The first is theory of mind: the social act of reading what another person intends, feels, and needs, and adjusting to it in real time. The second is flexible dexterity: non-routine physical manipulation, the hands-and-senses work that resists being scripted. The third is vision and strategy: the capacity to set goals worth pursuing and direct effort toward them.

Physical tasks rank hardest to automate for a blunt economic reason — building hardware with sensors and complex moving parts is expensive. But the deeper point is about direction. As the researcher Jennifer Steele writes, "The ability to set and direct tasks toward goals (guided by vision/strategy) and with human rationales (informed by theory of mind) is what makes automation a complement to rather than a substitute for the task" (Steele, 2024). That is the whole game in one sentence. AI is astonishing at execution. It is empty on purpose. Someone still has to decide what is worth making, and for whom, and why.

AI is astonishing at execution and empty on purpose. Someone still has to decide what is worth making, and for whom, and why. That someone is you.

The Economics of What's Scarce

Here is where this stops being philosophy and becomes a paycheck. The first measurable effect of AI on labor has not been mass layoffs — it has been wage compression. Workers in high-AI-exposure occupations saw real wage growth fall roughly 6.7 points behind their low-exposure peers after 2023, with service occupations hit hardest. When AI captures the value of routine execution, the price of that execution falls. And scarcity moves to whatever the machine cannot supply.

The market is already repricing accordingly. In the WEF's survey of over a thousand employers, the top five core skills workers need are analytical thinking, resilience and flexibility, leadership and social influence, creative thinking, and motivation and self-awareness — human-centered abilities, nearly all of them. Leadership and social influence rose 22 percentage points in importance in two years; creative thinking rose 17 (World Economic Forum, 2025). Seventy-seven percent of employers say they plan to reskill their existing workforce to work alongside AI by 2030, while half of executives name a shortage of exactly these skills as the top barrier to adopting AI at all (World Economic Forum, 2025). Read that twice. The bottleneck to deploying the machines is the humans who can direct them.

The Comfortable Story Has a Shelf Life

I refuse to sell you a fairy tale, because the evidence includes a warning. The same MIT paper that maps the rising tide shows the average failure rate roughly halving every two to three years — so "move toward tasks AI finds hard" is advice with a decaying half-life. And the WEF concedes its own caveat: advances in robotics, and the fusion of generative AI with robotic systems, could erode the physical-dexterity and sensory bottlenecks that look impregnable today. "No substitution potential" was a verdict about today's models, scored by one of them.

But "the bottleneck may narrow later" is not "the bottleneck is gone now." And the rising-tide framing cuts both ways. Precisely because the tide rises gradually instead of crashing, workers and institutions have a real — if closing — window to build the human-centered and hybrid skills employers are already scrambling to hire. The claim was never that these skills are eternally automation-proof. It is that they are where scarce human value concentrates right now, and where deliberate investment pays off first.

What to Build on the Human Side

So here is the concrete first step, the one I took myself. Pick one thing you do that requires you to be in the room — reading a client, mentoring someone, shaping a rough idea into a direction, working with your hands — and start treating it as your craft, not your overhead. Give the routine execution to the agents. I do. Then pour the hours you reclaim into the part the machine flagged as unautomatable: the judgment, the empathy, the vision of what is worth making.

I came to AI to lower the cost of making things that matter, and it has. But the tools only amplify a direction someone chooses. The skills that remain human are not a consolation prize for the automated age. They are the steering wheel. Take hold of it while the window is open.

A category of work that resists automation, forming a durable limit on what machines can take over. Research converges on three: social perception, physical dexterity, and goal-setting.
Theory of mind
The human ability to infer another person's beliefs, intentions, and feelings and adjust behavior accordingly — the social perspective-taking that anchors empathy and negotiation.
Flexible dexterity
Non-routine physical manipulation guided by the senses, difficult to automate because it demands costly hardware and real-time sensory judgment.
Vision and strategy
The capacity to set worthwhile goals and direct effort toward them, giving execution its purpose and making AI a complement rather than a substitute.
Rising tide
A model of AI automation as a broad, gradual advance across many tasks at once, rather than a sudden "crashing wave" that eliminates one job category overnight.
Wage compression
The narrowing or lagging of wages in AI-exposed occupations as the market value of routine execution falls, even before layoffs occur.
