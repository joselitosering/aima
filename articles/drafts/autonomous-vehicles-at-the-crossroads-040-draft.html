I have spent most of my working life betting on tools — small tools, in small hands, doing what teams and budgets used to do. So when a study lands claiming that a machine now drives more safely than I do, I don't reach for wonder. I reach for the footnotes. And the footnotes on autonomous vehicles, as of mid-2026, tell two stories at once: a genuine breakthrough, and a measurement system too weak to prove it. Both are true. That is exactly why this moment matters.

The number that stopped me

In July 2026, the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety — not a carmaker, not a startup with a valuation to defend — published an independent study finding that Waymo's driverless operation was involved in 68% fewer police-reportable crashes per mile than human drivers across San Francisco, Phoenix, Los Angeles and Austin (IIHS 2026). The same study found 85% fewer single-vehicle crashes and 81% fewer injury crashes per mile. I have read a lot of transportation research written to flatter someone's stock price. This did not read that way. It read like a craftsman's honest measurement of a tool that, on a limited scale, works.

What "68% fewer" actually measures

Here is the mechanism, demystified, because the headline hides the scale. Over the 2021–2024 study window Waymo logged about 50 million driverless miles against roughly 222 billion human-driven miles in the same cities. Only 89 of Waymo's crash involvements qualified as police-reportable, and just 64 of those happened in fully driverless operation (IIHS 2026). Waymo's own research, spanning 56.7 million rider-only miles, points the same direction — statistically fewer injury and airbag-deployment crashes than human benchmarks (Waymo 2025). But that second study is company-funded, and I refuse to launder a company's self-report into a neutral fact. The honest version is this: one careful operator, on a small footprint, is beating the human baseline. That is a real achievement. It is not yet an industry.

The peril the promise obscures

Now the other story. Tesla disclosed 17 autonomous-system incidents to federal regulators during its Austin robotaxi pilot between July 2025 and March 2026 — thirteen property-damage-only, one minor injury, and two that happened while a remote teleoperator had taken control of the car (Electrek 2026). That last detail should give anyone pause: the human backup, the thing meant to catch the machine's mistakes, was itself at the wheel when things went wrong. Scrutiny is broadening, too. NHTSA opened an investigation into 16 crashes involving Avride robotaxis in Dallas and Austin, citing alleged failures like changing lanes into oncoming traffic and striking stationary objects (NHTSA/Autoblog 2026). A contested Fortune analysis went further, arguing that "by Tesla's own math" its robotaxis drive several times worse than humans (Fortune 2026) — but that math rests on a tiny, redacted crash count, and I won't hand you a shaky number to win an argument.

The data gap is the real story

Here is what almost no one leads with, and what I think is the actual news. The safety claims — good and bad — cannot be trusted at scale yet, because the instrument we use to measure them is broken. NHTSA's Standing General Order, the only national tool tracking self-driving crashes, was built to enforce recalls, not to compare crash rates. Companies must report crashes but are not required to report the miles they drive. Only Waymo voluntarily publishes its mileage, which means no true per-mile crash rate exists for Cruise, Zoox, Tesla or the rest. IIHS researchers had to throw out roughly a quarter of reported crashes as redundant or non-qualifying just to build a rough comparison (IIHS 2026). We are flying a new aircraft with no altimeter and calling the smooth first minutes proof of safety.

On a limited scale, these driverless cars are safer than human drivers — who can be impaired or drowsy or suffer lapses in attention. But the present data collection system isn't good enough to allow continuous monitoring of a large-scale expansion. — David Harkey, President, IIHS (2026)

Why this is not just a car story

I write about AI as a creative instrument — a way to lower the cost of making something that matters, and to put capability in hands that were priced out of it. Autonomous driving is the same story wearing a heavier coat. A machine that never gets drunk, drowsy, or distracted could return mobility to the elderly, the disabled, and the working parent who cannot afford a second car. Those stakes are human before they are technical. Which is exactly why the measurement gap is not a wonky footnote — it is the moral center of the thing. As IIHS's Eric Teoh put it, the early signs are encouraging, but "now we need to get the data collection system right, so that we can ensure that level of safety continues as these technologies become more prevalent" (IIHS 2026). We do not get to skip that step because the demo felt magical.

The call to build

So here is the concrete first step, and it is not "trust the robot" or "ban the robot." It is: demand the altimeter. Mileage disclosure should be mandatory, not voluntary — every operator, every mile, in a public database designed to compare rather than merely to recall. If you build, cover, invest in, or regulate this technology, that is the single reform that turns a promising anecdote into verifiable safety. The 68% figure may well hold as deployment scales. I hope it does; I'd ride in that car. But hope is not a methodology. Build the instrument that can prove it, and the promise stops being a bet. That is the crossroads — and we are the ones holding the map.

Driverless operation
A mode in which the automated system controls the vehicle with no human driver behind the wheel — the condition under which most of Waymo's studied crashes occurred.
Police-reportable
A crash serious enough to legally require a police report, typically involving injury or significant property damage — the threshold IIHS used to compare crash rates.
Robotaxi
A self-driving vehicle operated as a ride-hailing service without a human driver, such as those piloted by Waymo, Tesla, and Avride.
Teleoperator
A remote human operator who can monitor or take control of an autonomous vehicle from a distance, serving as a backup to the automated system.
Standing General Order
NHTSA's mandate requiring companies to report crashes involving automated driving systems; built to enforce recalls, it does not require reporting the miles driven.
Per-mile crash rate
The number of crashes divided by miles traveled — the only fair way to compare safety across operators, and impossible to compute without mileage disclosure.
