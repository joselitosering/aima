I did not expect a study to keep me up at night, but this one did. In May of 2026, Google DeepMind published a trial in Nature Medicine in which its diagnostic AI, a system called AMIE, was judged more empathetic and more trustworthy than human primary-care physicians — beating them on 29 of 32 measured axes across 105 cases. Read that again. Not more accurate. More caring. I have spent years arguing that AI is an instrument in the hands of people who make things, a way to lower the cost of creating something that matters. But an instrument that comforts you better than your own doctor is a different kind of tool, and it forces a question I could not put down: can a machine that feels nothing actually learn to care?

The Night a Machine Out-Comforted the Doctors

Here is the honest caveat that makes the finding more interesting, not less. The patients in the AMIE trial were not sick people in a real clinic. They were trained actors typing into a chat window, and the "empathy" they rated was the empathy they felt reading text on a screen (Tu et al., Nature Medicine). Real-world outcome data has not been published. So the study did not prove that a machine cares. It proved something stranger and more unsettling: that a machine can perform care convincingly enough that human beings prefer the performance. I have built enough with AI to know the difference between a thing that works and a thing that is what it appears to be. The gap between those two is exactly where this whole debate lives.

How a Machine Performs Feeling

Let me demystify the mechanism, because the mystery is where the hype hides. The field that makes this possible is called affective computing, and it was founded at MIT by Rosalind Picard in the 1990s. What these systems do is emotional AI in the most literal sense: they pattern-match. They read the words you type, the tremor in your voice, the shape of your face, and they map those signals onto labels — happy, anxious, grieving — then generate a response statistically likely to soothe. There is no inner weather behind it. Picard, the woman who invented the field, could not be blunter. In a 2026 interview she said of these systems, "It's not thinking, it's not learning, it's not feeling. It's not experiencing anything like our thoughts, feelings, mind, experience" (Picard, Gospel Coalition). A companion chatbot that tells you it understands your loneliness has no idea what loneliness is. It has only ever seen the word.

The Empathy Economy

None of this is a thought experiment for a seminar room. Real money has already decided the question is worth billions. The market for machines that read and answer human feeling is not coming; it is here and compounding.

The demand for artificial closeness is not a fringe. It is a market the size of a national economy, and it is growing at more than thirty percent a year.

It Works. That's the Problem — and the Promise.

Here is where I have to argue against my own instinct, because the strongest case is not the one I want to be true. It is called functionalism, and it goes like this: if performed empathy reliably comforts, heals, and reduces suffering, then the absence of a feeling behind it may simply not matter. Picard herself concedes the tension. Recalling a clinician trained in formulaic active listening, she noted, "They're empathizing without feeling anything, but it works. It's effective" (Picard, Gospel Coalition). Human empathy is often partly performed too — the practiced bedside manner, the therapist's trained attention. We do not deny those their value. And for a grieving person at 3 a.m., a machine that is always awake, never judges, and never tires may deliver more consistent comfort than the scarce, imperfect human attention actually available to them.

A machine that comforts you better than your doctor is not a shortcut around the human. It is a mirror held up to how thinly we have been spreading the human all along.

What We Learn From Machines That Feel Nothing

And yet. The philosopher Sherry Turkle, who has studied what she calls the relational artifact since the 1970s, draws the line I keep coming back to. "Generative AI does not care," she writes, "in the way humans use the word 'care.'... Empathy is the capacity to put yourself in someone else's place and a commitment to stay the course. Chatbots have not lived a human life" (Turkle). Her deepest worry is not that machines will feel. It is that we will stop noticing they don't. If we call the performance empathy, she warns, "we downgrade empathy" itself. A 2024 Nature Machine Intelligence paper framed it through Robert Nozick's experience machine — the old thought experiment asking whether a perfectly simulated good feeling is as valuable as the real thing (Shteynberg et al.). We learn how to care by being cared for, by other humans, imperfectly. Teach a generation to practice intimacy on something that has none, and you do not expand human capacity. You risk quietly hollowing it out.

The Instrument, Not the Substitute

So where does a builder land? Not in refusal — that ship has sailed, and some of these tools genuinely help. Picard's own FDA-cleared seizure-detection algorithm supports human flourishing precisely because it is narrow, reviewed, and honest about what it is. The danger is not the tool. It is the substitution — mistaking the instrument for the musician, the performance for the person. My rule is simple and I would offer it to you as the first concrete step: use emotional AI to reach people you could never otherwise reach, never to replace the people already reaching for each other. Build the companion chatbot that gets a lonely elder through the night — then use it to schedule the visit from a human who will actually stay. Let the machine lower the cost of contact. Never let it become the whole of contact. Machines cannot learn to care. But we can learn, with their help, to care at a scale and a reach we could not manage alone — if we never forget which one of us is doing the caring.

Affective computing
The field, founded by Rosalind Picard at MIT, dedicated to building systems that detect, interpret, and respond to human emotion through signals like text, voice, and facial expression.
Emotional AI
Software that recognizes emotional cues and generates emotionally appropriate responses by statistical pattern-matching — labeling a feeling without experiencing one.
Companion chatbot
A conversational app such as Replika or Xiaoice marketed as a friend, confidant, or partner, designed to simulate ongoing emotional connection with a user.
Functionalism
The philosophical position that what matters about a mental state is what it does, not what it feels like inside — implying that empathy which reliably comforts may count as empathy regardless of inner experience.
Relational artifact
Sherry Turkle's term for a machine engineered to elicit and sustain human emotional attachment, from early robotic toys to modern companion bots.
Experience machine
Robert Nozick's 1974 thought experiment asking whether a life of perfectly simulated pleasant experiences is as valuable as a real one — a lens for judging simulated empathy.
AMIE
Google DeepMind's multimodal diagnostic AI which, in a 2026 Nature Medicine trial, was rated more empathetic and trustworthy than human physicians by patient actors in simulation.
