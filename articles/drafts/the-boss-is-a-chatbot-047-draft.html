Somewhere in an office park right now, a worker is being scored — keystrokes counted, idle minutes logged, the cadence of her replies timed — by a system she has never seen and cannot question. This is not a forecast. In the United States, 90% of firms already run at least one algorithmic tool to instruct, monitor or evaluate their workers, according to a 2025 OECD survey of more than 6,000 mid-level managers (OECD, 2025). So the open question is no longer whether the algorithm manages you. It does. The question is whether the same system that counts your keystrokes should also be permitted to end your livelihood — quietly, unaccountably, and without a human ever reading the file.

Ninety Percent Is Not a Typo

The OECD figure deserves a second look, because the instinct is to assume a decimal slipped. It didn't. Adoption averages 79% across France, Germany, Italy and Spain, and 40% in Japan; more than three-quarters of US managers say their firm offers ten or more of the fifteen tools the survey measured (OECD, 2025). This is algorithmic management — the use of software to assign, watch, rate and direct workers — and it is no longer a Silicon Valley curiosity. It is the water the modern workplace swims in.

Firms have, so far, been cautious about the cruelest end of the spectrum. The OECD found that sanctioning tools — software that penalizes poor performance — were adopted by just 14% of firms, against 23% for tools that reward it (OECD, 2025). Restraint, of a kind. But 14% is not zero, and restraint is not a right. It is a mood, and moods change with the quarterly numbers.

The Thing Nobody Says at the All-Hands

Here is the sentence the vendor demo skips: the machine that manages you is being built to manage without you. Algorithmic management sells itself as decision support — a tidy dashboard for a busy human boss. But a dashboard that ranks fifty people from best to worst has already made the decision; the human just clicks. Veena Dubal, the law professor who coined the term algorithmic wage discrimination, describes a practice in which "individual workers are paid different hourly wages — calculated with ever-changing formulas using granular data on location, individual behavior, demand, supply, or other factors — for broadly similar work" (Dubal, 2023). Pay was the pilot program. Firing is the sequel.

That firms already convert worker data into leverage is not speculation. In high-exposure occupations, real wage growth fell roughly 6.7 percentage points behind low-exposure work after 2023, and the bottom wage quartile fell 10.7% behind (Anthropic Economic Index whitepaper, 2026). Before the layoff comes the squeeze — and the squeeze is measurable now.

Where the Firing Already Happens

If you want to see the fully automated version, look at the gig economy, where the euphemism for "fired" is deactivation. Human Rights Watch, in its 2025 report The Gig Trap, surveyed 127 platform workers in Texas: 40 said they had been deactivated at least once, and 65 said they were "fearful" or "very fearful" of it (HRW, 2025). The report's authors put it without decoration: workers are "assigned orders, supervised, paid, and fired by algorithms" (HRW, 2025).

And here is the figure that should end the argument. Of the workers who were deactivated, nearly half said the platform later cleared them of any wrongdoing (HRW, 2025). Read that twice. An automated system severed people's income, and roughly one in two of those decisions was wrong — discovered only because the worker clawed back through an appeals process that barely exists. This is a Texas sample of 127, not a national rate; treat it as testimony, not census. But testimony this consistent is evidence.

An automated system severed people's income, and roughly half of those firings turned out to be mistakes. The boss was a chatbot, and the chatbot was wrong.

"A Human Signs Off" and Other Comforting Fictions

The defenders are not fools, and they are not entirely wrong. In the OECD survey, 60% of managers said the tools improved the quality of their own decisions, and many reported higher, not lower, job satisfaction (OECD, 2025). Sanctioning tools remain the least-adopted category. The reassurance offered is that a person is always the last word — the human in the loop, the manager who reviews the machine's recommendation before anyone is dismissed.

Press on that phrase and it buckles. A human handed an opaque score she cannot interrogate, on a deadline, with no incentive to overrule the expensive system her employer bought — that is not oversight. That is a signature. Due process — the worker's right to know the charge, see the evidence, and contest the decision — does not survive contact with a model whose reasoning is a trade secret. The EU's own researchers warn that these systems track "location, keystrokes, and even biometric data like voice and facial expressions," shifting power decisively toward management (EPRS, 2025). Worker exposure across the EU is projected to climb from 42.3% to 55.5% (EPRS, 2025). The trend line does not bend toward more human judgment.

We have a preview of the traditional-workplace version, too. After IBM Japan introduced Watson-based "compensation advisor" software, unionized employees' individual performance rate averaged 63.6% against 100% for everyone else in one 2019 bonus assessment — many union members were scored at zero (Dubal, 2023). The machine did not invent that bias. It laundered it, and returned it wearing the clean shirt of objectivity.

The Question You Now Have to Carry

Here is the discomfort I can't resolve for you. The danger is not that algorithms are cruel; cruelty at least implies attention. The danger is that they are indifferent, and indifference administered at scale is its own quiet species of violence. A human manager who fires you unjustly can, at minimum, be made to look you in the eye. A system cannot be shamed, cannot be appealed to, cannot be asked why — and increasingly, per the OECD's own map of which functions are being automated, is not designed to answer.

So the choice in front of every workplace is not whether to use these tools. That ship has sailed, and 90% of firms were on it. The choice is narrower and harder: whether a decision that ends a person's income must be one a human being can be held accountable for having made. Right now the law mostly says no, the vendors say trust us, and the error rate says roughly half. When the boss is a chatbot, the only real question left is who you call when it's wrong — and whether anyone is obligated to pick up.

Algorithmic management
The use of software and automated systems to assign, monitor, rate and direct workers — the managerial functions once performed by a human supervisor, now partly handled by code.
Sanctioning tools
Algorithmic tools that penalize poor performance (through pay cuts, restricted access, discipline or termination), as distinct from tools that reward good performance. The OECD found these adopted by just 14% of firms.
Algorithmic wage discrimination
Coined by law professor Veena Dubal: paying individual workers different hourly wages for broadly similar work, set by opaque, constantly shifting formulas drawing on granular personal and behavioral data.
Deactivation
The platform-economy euphemism for being fired — an automated cutoff of a worker's access to a gig platform, often issued with no explanation and no meaningful route of appeal.
Human in the loop
A design or governance claim that a person reviews an automated recommendation before a consequential decision is executed. Its protective value depends entirely on whether that person can actually interrogate and overrule the system.
Due process
The procedural right to know the charge against you, see the evidence, and contest a decision before a neutral party — a safeguard that erodes when the decision-maker's reasoning is a proprietary, unexaminable model.
