In March 2026, FBI Director Kash Patel sat before the Senate Intelligence Committee and, under questioning from Senator Ron Wyden, said the quiet part out loud: the Bureau buys Americans' location data. No warrant. No probable cause. No judge in the room. Just a purchase order, the kind any advertiser could file, for the same trail of pings your weather app harvested off your phone last Tuesday (TechCrunch). His predecessor denied this practice existed in 2023. Nothing changed between the denial and the confession except who was willing to say it in public. That's not a scandal breaking. That's a scandal that had been operating in daylight, waiting for someone to stop calling it a secret.

THE CONFESSION THAT WASN'T NEWS TO ANYONE WHO BUILT THE SYSTEM

Here is what nobody in that hearing room said out loud, because everyone already knew it: the Fourth Amendment was never repealed. It was routed around. The government did not need to win an argument about warrantless searches — it only needed a vendor willing to sell what a search would have found. Venntel, a subsidiary of Gravy Analytics, and Babel Street, whose Locate X product is marketed like a car dealership's upgrade package, have supplied cell-phone location data to DHS, ICE, CBP, and the FBI since at least 2017 (ACLU). Nine years is not a loophole. Nine years is infrastructure.

Patel's testimony wasn't a leak or a whistleblow. It was volunteered, calmly, as operational fact — because by 2026 the practice was so normalized inside the agency that denying it, as Christopher Wray had in 2023, had become the more awkward position to defend (Washington Times). The reversal is the real story, and it isn't really about Kash Patel. It's about what it means when an agency can graduate from lying about mass surveillance to shrugging about it in three years, and face no consequence for either version.

FOLLOW THE PING: HOW THE PIPELINE ACTUALLY WORKS

The mechanism is insultingly mundane. A weather app, a mobile game, a ride-share app asks for your location "to improve your experience," and you tap agree because the alternative is not using the app. That ping doesn't stay in the app. It enters the real-time advertising bid-stream — the automated auction where thousands of ad-tech firms glimpse your location for a fraction of a second to decide whether your attention is worth bidding on. Data brokers sit inside that auction not to buy an ad, but to harvest and aggregate the pings themselves, building a queryable, timestamped record of everywhere your phone has been.

Then a federal agency buys a subscription to query that record — no individualized suspicion required, because the government's position is that it isn't searching you, it's shopping for what officials call commercially available information, or CAI. In February 2026, DHS layered a $1 billion contract with Palantir on top, fusing that purchased data with AI analytics across every component, ICE and CBP included (The Register; ACLU).

THE LOOPHOLE HAS A NAME, AND ITS DEFENDERS SAY IT PROUDLY

Ask the practice's defenders to justify it and they don't hide behind vague national-security language — they cite doctrine. Senator Tom Cotton put it plainly: "The keywords are commercially available — if any other person can buy it and the FBI can buy it, and it helps them locate a depraved child molester or savage cartel leader, I would certainly hope the FBI is doing anything they can to keep Americans safe" (FedScoop). The legal architecture underneath that sentiment is the Supreme Court's third-party doctrine, which holds that you have no reasonable expectation of privacy in information you voluntarily handed to a third party — a bank, a phone carrier, a weather app. Combine that doctrine with a marketplace willing to aggregate and resell what you handed over, and the warrant requirement doesn't get violated. It gets bypassed.

The ACLU's framing, from FOIA documents released in January 2026, is the sharpest rebuttal on record: "The Fourth Amendment generally requires the government to get a warrant before searching your private information, but government agencies are circumventing the intent of the Constitution by simply buying sensitive, personal data from private companies" (ACLU). That sentence doesn't dispute that the data is technically available on the open market. It disputes that scale and aggregation are constitutionally neutral just because each isolated point is.

THE VOTE NOBODY REMEMBERS TAKING

This is the structural part, the part that isn't an accident: Congress already decided this matters. On April 17, 2024, the House passed the Fourth Amendment Is Not For Sale Act 219 to 199 — 96 Democrats and 123 Republicans agreeing, across a chasm that agrees on nothing else, that a court order should be required before the government buys Americans' data from brokers (GovTrack). That bill has never passed the Senate. Two years later, Representatives Thomas Massie and Lauren Boebert introduced a companion bill timed to the April 2026 FISA Section 702 reauthorization deadline — the last practical vehicle to close the loophole (Tech Policy Press). It stalled alongside Wyden's Government Surveillance Reform Act, reintroduced with the warning that "advances in technology, from AI to the explosion of Americans' data available for purchase, have far outpaced the laws protecting Americans' privacy and civil liberties" (Wyden). A bipartisan majority voted yes once. The practice continued anyway.

The government did not need to win the argument about your Fourth Amendment rights. It just needed a receipt.

— Dawn Ginhaua, AIMA

WHO, EXACTLY, AGREED TO THIS?

I want to be precise about what I'm not arguing. I am not arguing that no legitimate law-enforcement use case exists for commercially available data — cartel leaders and child predators are real, and Cotton's defense isn't a strawman. I'm arguing that a defense built on individual cases cannot justify an infrastructure built for population-scale collection: the distinction between "the government caught a bad actor" and "the government purchased a queryable movement history of everyone" is exactly what a 219-199 House majority already told us matters. DHS's FY2026 budget runs $165.59 billion across six components — overall departmental scale, not a surveillance line item, but worth holding next to the $1 billion Palantir contract and the "millions" in broker purchases the ACLU has documented since 2017 (Congress.gov).

That's what an agency can spend when buying data is cheaper, faster, and legally frictionless than doing the constitutionally accountable version of the same job. So here is the discomfort I'd like you to carry out of this piece, because it doesn't resolve cleanly. If the government can lawfully buy the same warrant-proof profile of you that no state privacy law can force a broker to stop selling, the question was never whether the Fourth Amendment applies to your phone. It's whether it still applies to anything you've ever handed a terms-of-service agreement — and whether "agreement" was ever the right word for what happened when you tapped "Allow" so a weather app would tell you if it might rain.

Glossary

Data Broker↑ Back to text

A company that collects, aggregates, and resells personal information about individuals — often without direct interaction with the people whose data it holds. Data brokers source raw data from apps, ad networks, public records, and other brokers, then package it into queryable profiles sold to advertisers, insurers, and, increasingly, government agencies. Venntel, Babel Street, and Fog Data Science are the three named repeatedly in federal contracting records as suppliers to DHS, ICE, CBP, and the FBI.

Bid-Stream (Real-Time Bidding)↑ Back to text

The automated auction system underlying most mobile and web advertising, in which a user's data — including location — is broadcast in real time to thousands of ad-tech companies who bid, within milliseconds, for the right to show that user an ad. Data brokers intercept and archive bid-stream data even when they never win a bid, turning a system designed to sell advertising into a surveillance-grade data-collection pipeline.

Third-Party Doctrine↑ Back to text

A legal principle, rooted in Supreme Court precedent, holding that individuals have no reasonable expectation of privacy in information voluntarily shared with a third party — a bank, a phone carrier, an app. It is the doctrinal foundation government agencies cite to justify buying data from brokers without a warrant, on the theory that no "search" occurs when the data was already handed to someone else.

Commercially Available Information (CAI)↑ Back to text

The official term federal agencies use to describe data purchased from brokers rather than obtained through legal process. Framing the data as "commercially available" is the legal maneuver that allows agencies to argue they are shopping, not searching — sidestepping Fourth Amendment warrant requirements that would otherwise apply to a comparable law-enforcement search.

Fourth Amendment Is Not For Sale Act↑ Back to text

A bipartisan federal bill (H.R. 4639) that would require government agencies to obtain a court order before purchasing Americans' data from brokers, closing the commercially-available-information loophole. Passed the House 219-199 on April 17, 2024, with support from 96 Democrats and 123 Republicans. Has not passed the Senate as of mid-2026.

Locate X↑ Back to text

A location-tracking product marketed by data broker Babel Street, sold as an add-on service enabling customers — including federal agencies — to query historical and near-real-time movement data tied to specific devices. Named in ACLU FOIA reporting as one of the products purchased by DHS and its components since at least 2017.

FOIA (Freedom of Information Act)↑ Back to text

The federal law allowing members of the public to request records from government agencies. Much of what is publicly known about DHS's and the FBI's data-broker purchasing practices — including the Venntel and Babel Street contracts — became public specifically because organizations like the ACLU filed FOIA requests and, in some cases, litigated to force disclosure.
