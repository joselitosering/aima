For a decade, the artificial intelligence industry enjoyed a legal privilege it never had to name out loud. When an algorithm caused harm — a rejected job applicant, a teenager routed toward despair, a confident lie dressed up as a search result — the defense wrote itself: no human made the decision, so no human is liable. Call it the accountability holiday. Across 2025 and 2026, courts, juries, and legislatures on both sides of the Atlantic have started mailing the industry its bill.

The Defense That Was Always a Category Error

"The algorithm did it" was never really a defense. It was a category error wearing a defense's clothes. An algorithm is not a moral agent who wandered off and did something regrettable; it is a product someone designed, tuned, deployed, and monetized. The autonomy of the system was offered as a firewall — the machine chose, not us — when autonomy is in fact the thing that was engineered.

Legal scholars have finally named this. In the Stanford CodeX "phantom agent" framework, Vanderbilt law professor Daniel Gervais and Stanford fellow John Nay argue that the autonomy question "dissolves once legal intent is understood functionally rather than metaphysically." Their line is the whole shift in eleven words: "The AI is the means of attribution, not its target" (Jones Walker LLP AI Law Blog, 2026). You do not have to prove the model is conscious. You have to ask who built it and who profited.

What the Juries Actually Did

Juries stopped waiting for the philosophy to settle. In March 2026, a Los Angeles jury returned a reported $6 million verdict against Meta and Google in K.G.M. v. Meta Platforms, Inc. and Google LLC — described in legal commentary as the first of its kind to hold social media companies liable for harm caused by algorithmic design, after a plaintiff argued that addictive features engineered into Facebook and YouTube caused her anxiety and depression as a minor (Solicitors Journal, 2026). The dollar figure and the "first of its kind" framing come from that commentary rather than the primary docket, so treat the number as reported, not verified.

Employment law moved first and moved further. In Mobley v. Workday, a federal judge allowed a discrimination suit to proceed against Workday as an "agent" of the employers using its hiring-screening tools — the first application of agency theory to hold an AI vendor directly liable for discriminatory hiring, later certified as a nationwide class covering applicants over 40 whom the software rejected, the lead plaintiff turned away "within minutes" across more than a hundred applications (Jones Walker LLP, 2025).

The Statute That Says the Quiet Part

Then California wrote the subtext into law. AB 316, signed October 13, 2025 and codified at Civil Code section 1714.46, provides that a defendant who "developed, modified, or used" AI may not defend a civil action on the ground that the system autonomously caused the harm. It is a direct statutory funeral for the accountability holiday. It is also, honestly, narrow: it preserves every ordinary defense — causation, foreseeability, comparative fault — and pointedly does not create strict liability (Jones Walker LLP, 2026). The legislature closed one escape hatch, not the building.

The same institution-not-instrument logic keeps recurring in the small cases too. When Air Canada argued in Moffatt that its website chatbot was a separate entity responsible for its own statements, a tribunal declined the invitation. A company owns what its deployed system tells the public — the phantom agent does not get to sign for the mistake.

"The algorithm did it" is the corporate equivalent of blaming the dog for eating the homework you trained it to eat, then billed the neighbors for.

Editors, Not Neutral Carriers

The most consequential move may be doctrinal. In July 2026, a German court held Google liable for inaccuracies in its AI search summaries, rejecting the claim that the company was a neutral pipe and finding that AI-generated overviews are editorial content (BotBeat, 2026). That revives the old carrier-versus-publisher distinction: a system that rewords, summarizes, and selects from sources is exercising editorial discretion, not merely indexing someone else's speech — which pushes it toward a publisher's liability and away from Section 230-style immunity. As one commentator put it, when AI systems "make substantive editorial choices about what information reaches users," they "must be held to the same accountability standards as human editors" (BotBeat, 2026).

The Liability Squeeze

Here is the part the triumphant headlines miss, and the part I cannot let the powerful off the hook for. Courts are expanding accountability at the exact moment vendors are contracting it away. A market study found that 88% of AI vendors cap their own liability, often at the monthly subscription fee, and only 17% warrant regulatory compliance (Jones Walker LLP, 2025). This is the liability squeeze: legal risk flows downhill, away from the well-resourced developer who trained the model and toward the small deployer who cannot audit it. The emerging theory asks whether a reasonable alternative design — better classifiers, warnings, human review, monitoring for the moment a launch-day guardrail decays — should have existed. That is the right question. Whether the answer lands on the party that could actually have built the safer system is another matter entirely.

The Question You Now Have to Carry

So the holiday is ending — in principle. Many of these outcomes are settlements that disclaim precedent; the proposed August 2026 Meta age-assurance settlement contains no admission of liability (Stanford Law/CodeX, 2026). Adjudicators are lawyers, not data scientists, asked to reconstruct opaque systems from the outside. As Bernhard Maier wrote of the tobacco parallel, the question is no longer whether comparable claims emerge from algorithmic harm but "when, and in what form" (Solicitors Journal, 2026). The law has finally stopped asking whether a person decided. It now asks whether the architecture was reasonably safe and how its makers responded to the evidence of harm. The uncomfortable question the rest of us inherit is simpler: when accountability finally arrives, will it reach the room where the system was built — or only the room where it was rented?

accountability holiday
The de facto period in which AI developers avoided liability by arguing that, because no human made the harmful decision, no human could be held responsible.
agency theory
A legal doctrine treating one party as acting on another's behalf; applied in Mobley v. Workday to hold an AI hiring vendor liable as the "agent" of the employers using it.
phantom agent
Stanford CodeX framing for an autonomous AI system treated as if it were an independent actor — a fiction the framework rejects, attributing its conduct back to the humans and institutions that deployed it.
carrier-versus-publisher distinction
The doctrinal line between a neutral conduit for third-party content (protected) and an editorial publisher (liable); AI summaries that reword and select information push toward the publisher side.
liability squeeze
The dynamic in which courts expand AI accountability while vendor contracts simultaneously push legal and financial risk onto downstream customers.
reasonable alternative design
A product-liability standard asking whether a safer, feasible design of the system should have existed and been used.
