A few weeks ago I watched a friend build an entire underwater city on his laptop in an afternoon. Not concept art. Not a mood board. A walkable, lit, breathing environment you could drop a camera into — the kind of thing that used to require a render farm, a VFX house, and a budget with a comma most of us have never seen. He is not a studio. He is one person with a good idea and a subscription. That afternoon reorganized something in my head, and it is the reason I keep returning to a single word: presence. We are leaving the era of the pixel — the flat, framed image you look at — and entering the era of presence, media you can stand inside. Artificial intelligence is the instrument making that leap affordable, and I want to be honest about both the wonder and the bill.

The Desk Where the Blockbuster Now Lives

The filmmaker Shekhar Kapur put it plainly this year: "We have arrived at a point technologically where you can realistically produce a film carrying the visual scale of a blockbuster like 'Avatar' for a fraction of the cost, right on your desktop." (Variety India, 2026) I used to read lines like that as marketing. I don't anymore, because I have watched it happen at my own desk. What Kapur is describing is not a better camera or a cheaper editing suite. It is the collapse of the entire capital stack that stood between a vision and its realization. The studio, the equipment, the crew of specialists — these were the gatekeepers, and the gate was money. When the cost of a cinematic-scale environment falls toward the cost of an idea, the question stops being "who can afford to build this?" and becomes "who has something worth building?" That is a different world, and we walked into it quietly.

How Presence Actually Gets Built

Let me demystify the mechanism, because "AI magic" is a phrase I refuse to use. Immersive media — the umbrella term for content you experience from the inside, in virtual reality, augmented reality, or spatial video game worlds — is expensive because every surface, sound, and behavior has to be authored by hand. AI attacks that cost in three concrete places. First, procedural content creation: instead of an artist modeling ten thousand trees, a system generates a whole forest from a described intent. Second, real-time rendering: the scene is drawn frame by frame as you move through it, so nothing is pre-baked and everything can respond. Third, automated QA testing — using AI agents to play a build over and over, hunting for what breaks. Capcom reportedly ran 30,000 hours of automated QA testing per month to catch bugs late in development. (Google Cloud, 2026) None of this is sorcery. It is labor, compressed. The intuition still comes from you; the drudgery is what gets absorbed.

When the Cost of Cinema Collapses

Here is the economic unlock, in numbers. The market for generative AI in virtual reality — the technology stack behind these AI-authored worlds — grew from $5.08 billion in 2025 to $6.73 billion in 2026, a 32.3% growth rate, and one widely-cited forecast projects it reaching $20.23 billion by 2030. (The Business Research Company, via GII Research, 2026 — one vendor's projection, not consensus.) Meanwhile global video game revenues have reached $252 billion a year, even as blockbuster titles can take up to a decade to ship. (Google Cloud, 2026) Read those two facts together and you see the pressure: enormous demand, punishing production timelines. AI is the release valve. When the cost of cinema and the cost of a game world collapse toward the price of a laptop and a monthly plan, the doors open for exactly the people who were priced out before — the diaspora creator, the one-person studio, the kid in a town with no film school.

Living Worlds, Not Just Prettier Pictures

The deeper shift is not visual — it is behavioral. Google Cloud calls the new form living games: "With the advances of AI, we can give systems a deep understanding of context and logic, enabling characters and environments to reason and adapt based entirely on what a player does." (Google Cloud, 2026) The examples are already arriving. Square Enix is adding a real-time conversational companion to Dragon Quest X Online; Ubisoft's "Teammates" prototype swaps command menus for AI squadmates you direct with your voice, and they react to your intent. This is what presence means in practice — a world that answers back. No wonder 89% of game developers say AI is changing what players expect from a game. (Google Cloud / The Harris Poll, 2026 — sample size undisclosed in the source.) The pixel was something you watched. The living world is something that watches you back and adjusts. That is a new grammar for storytelling, and we are all still learning to speak it.

The pixel was something you looked at. Presence is something you stand inside — and increasingly, something that stands there with you.

The Honest Ledger

I promised an honest accounting, so here it is. When the price of cinematic-scale content collapses, it does not only free creators — it undercuts the artists, actors, VFX crews, and voice performers whose labor defined the craft. Abundance can become a flood of frictionless, personalized content that is plentiful and creatively thin. Even Kapur, an optimist, holds the line: "intuition and innovation remain fundamentally human qualities." The risk is real that this technology displaces skilled workers faster than it creates new roles. I don't wave that away. A tool that lowers the cost of making something also lowers the cost of making something soulless, and presence generated on demand can feel hollow when there is no human intention behind it. The instrument is neutral. What we build with it is not. Anyone who sells you only the upside is selling you a pitch deck, not the truth.

Start With One Scene

So what do you do with this? Not "learn AI" — too vague. Do this: pick one scene you have always wanted to make and could never afford. One. A single environment, a single moment of presence. Build it this month with the tools already on your desk, and pay attention to where the machine handles the labor and where your judgment does the real work. Kapur believes "the younger generation will almost certainly make far better films than I ever did — that is a statistical guarantee," because thousands of people now hold the tools who never held them before. I believe him. The lesson I took from watching that underwater city rise in an afternoon is not that the machine is a genius. It is that the barrier was never talent. It was cost — and the cost just fell. Build the scene. See what you're actually made of.
