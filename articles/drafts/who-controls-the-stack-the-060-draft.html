Last month I made a short music video in an afternoon — scored, cut, color-graded — on a laptop, a cup of coffee, and a stack of AI tools that a decade ago would have needed a studio, a crew, and a month. It felt like sovereignty. Then I did the honest math on where every one of those tools actually runs, and I stopped feeling sovereign. I don't own the miracle. I rent it. And the landlord is a supply chain so concentrated that a few companies, in a few places, quietly decide what the rest of us are allowed to make.

The Day I Realized I Was Renting My Own Miracle

Here is the thing nobody tells you when you fall in love with these tools: the model is the least interesting part of the power structure. We argue endlessly about which large language model is smartest this week, as if the winner of that race wins everything. It doesn't. Underneath every model — mine, yours, OpenAI's — sits a physical and economic layer cake I've started calling the stack: the chain of hardware, factories, software, and rented computers that has to exist before a single token of text or frame of video can be generated. Whoever controls the stack controls the revolution. Not the person with the cleverest prompt.

I say this as a practitioner, not a policy analyst. The reason it matters to someone building on a shoestring is simple. If the foundational layers are owned by a handful of players, then the price, the availability, and even the permission to create flow downhill from decisions I have no vote in. So I went looking for who actually holds the taps. What I found was less a competitive market than a series of magnificent bottlenecks.

Five Layers, Almost No Competition

Read the stack from the bottom up and the concentration is almost comic. At the equipment layer, the machines that etch the most advanced chips rely on EUV lithography — extreme ultraviolet lithography — and exactly one company on Earth, ASML in the Netherlands, sells them. At the manufacturing layer sits the foundry, the fabrication plant that turns silicon designs into physical chips; more than 90% of the most advanced chips come from a single company, TSMC, on a single island, Taiwan (Berkeley GSPP, "An Evolving AI Supply Chain," 2025). At the chip layer, NVIDIA held over 90% of the market for data-center GPUs used in AI in 2025, with AMD at roughly 4% (Berkeley GSPP). Then comes the layer people forget: software. NVIDIA's CUDA, its proprietary programming platform, is the default across every major AI framework, which means even a competitive rival chip struggles to matter, because the developer world is already fluent in one company's tongue.

Finally, the top layer, the one I actually touch — compute. Most of the world's AI runs on rented capacity from three hyperscaler cloud providers: Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud together held about 63% of the global cloud market as of Q2 2025 (Berkeley GSPP). Five layers, and at nearly every one the number of real players you could count on one hand — with fingers left over.

Chris Miller, the Tufts professor who wrote Chip War, put the interdependence bluntly: "Today there's not a single country in the world that can produce, not even an advanced chip, but a moderately advanced chip on its own" (Miller, Carnegie Mellon, 2026). No nation controls the whole stack. But a very small number of companies control the pieces that no one can route around.

The Economics of a Chokepoint

Here is why concentration is not an abstraction. When supply is scarce and demand is infinite, the people who own the scarce thing get to write the future in advance. Look at the neoclouds — specialized GPU-rental companies that emerged to sit between the hyperscalers and everyone hungry for compute. One of them, CoreWeave, locked in a $22.4 billion GPU-capacity deal with OpenAI and a $14.2 billion deal with Meta (Berkeley GSPP). Read that again. Compute for the next several years is being pre-committed, in tens of billions, before the rest of us have even asked for a slot.

We argue about which model is smartest this week, as if the winner wins everything. But the model is the least interesting part of the power structure. Whoever controls the stack controls the revolution.

For a solo builder, this is the whole game. The reason my afternoon music video was even possible is that inference — the cost of running a finished model — has been falling fast. But that cost is set upstream, by whoever owns the chips and the fabs and the clouds. When they raise the rent, they raise it on all of us at once, and there is no second landlord to call. Concentration doesn't just create risk; it creates a single point of failure. Miller warns the whole system is "highly vulnerable to a devastating earthquake in Taiwan," because leading-edge fabrication lives in one seismic zone. The revolution I love has a fault line running under it.

Who This Locks Out — and Who It Could Free

I care about this because of who gets left standing outside the door. The promise of these tools — the reason I write about them at all — is that they collapse the cost of making something that matters, and hand creative power to people who were never going to raise venture money: the diaspora entrepreneur, the one-person studio, the kid in a province with a good idea and a bad connection. That promise is real. But it is a downstream promise, and it is only as durable as the upstream stack allows.

This is why nations are no longer content to rent. The push for cloud sovereignty — a country's insistence on owning domestic compute and data infrastructure rather than depending on foreign hyperscalers — is spreading fast, and it rhymes with what individual creators feel in miniature. China's "Big Fund" launched a $47 billion third phase in 2024 to build homegrown semiconductor capability in response to US-led export controls (Berkeley GSPP). And by mid-2025 that control regime had already loosened, with a licensing-and-fees system permitting some previously restricted NVIDIA and AMD exports to China (Berkeley GSPP) — proof that the chokepoints are political, negotiable, and therefore contestable. Even Chinese labs advancing under chip restrictions show the moat is not infinite.

The Stack Is Being Contested — So Contest It

The good news is that the picture is not frozen. The hyperscalers are shipping their own custom chips — Google's TPUs, Amazon's Trainium and Inferentia — to loosen their dependence on NVIDIA. Each of these is an ASIC, an application-specific integrated circuit designed for one job rather than general use, and every one of them chips away at a monopoly. A whole class of neocloud providers — specialized GPU-focused clouds like CoreWeave and Lambda Labs — now competes to break the big three's grip on access. The stack is being contested. That means our job is to contest it too.

So here is the concrete first step, the one I'm taking myself. Stop treating the stack as weather — something that just happens to you — and start treating it as a supply chain you can choose within. Learn where your tools actually run. Keep your creative work portable: build so you can move between a hyperscaler and a neocloud without rewriting your life. Support the open-model and open-tooling projects that refuse to depend on a single vendor's software lock-in, because a healthy alternative to CUDA is worth more to independent creators than any one clever app. Miller's most hopeful line is also a instruction: "As costs of AI decline, we're just going to put more of these capabilities in an ever-larger number of our devices." The cost is falling toward us. What we do with that falling cost — whether we use it to build something that outlives us or just to consume faster — is the one part of the stack we fully control. Own that layer. It's the only one they can't rent back to you.

the stack
The full chain of hardware, manufacturing, software, and rented compute that must exist before any AI model can run — from lithography machines up to cloud services.
EUV lithography
Extreme ultraviolet lithography, the precision process used to etch the most advanced chips. The machines are sold by only one company worldwide, ASML.
foundry
A semiconductor fabrication plant that manufactures chips from designs. Leading-edge foundry capacity is dominated by TSMC in Taiwan.
CUDA
NVIDIA's proprietary software platform for programming its GPUs. Its maturity makes it the developer default and a powerful competitive moat.
hyperscaler
A giant cloud provider operating at massive scale — Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud — that rents out most of the world's AI compute.
neocloud
A specialized cloud company focused on renting GPU capacity for AI, such as CoreWeave or Lambda Labs, emerging to compete with the hyperscalers.
ASIC
An application-specific integrated circuit, a chip built for a single task; hyperscaler examples include Google's TPU and Amazon's Trainium and Inferentia.
cloud sovereignty
A nation's drive to control domestic compute and data infrastructure rather than depend on foreign-owned hyperscalers.
