In 2023, Sal Khan stood on a TED stage and made a promise big enough to reorder my sense of what was possible. AI, he said, was about to deliver "probably the biggest positive transformation that education has ever seen" by giving every student on the planet "an artificially intelligent but amazing personal tutor" (Khan, TED 2023). I believed him. I still do — but not in the uncomplicated way I did then. Three years later, Khan looked back at Khanmigo, the tutoring chatbot he built to make that promise real, and delivered a verdict that should stop every AI optimist cold: "For a lot of students, it was a non-event. They just didn't use it much" (Barnum, Chalkbeat). That gap — between the promise and the non-event — is the most honest place to start.

The Promise, and the Walk-Back

The dream is old and specific. In 1984, Benjamin Bloom described the two-sigma problem: students tutored one-on-one outperformed classroom students by roughly two standard deviations — a staggering gap that has haunted education ever since, precisely because no society can afford a private tutor for every child. Khan's 2023 claim was that AI had finally made that arithmetic collapse. For the first time, the marginal cost of a patient, always-available tutor could approach zero.

Then reality filed its report. Khan Academy's own chief learning officer, Kristen DiCerbo, put it plainly in 2026: "So far I am not seeing the revolution in education" (Barnum, Chalkbeat). The company is now redesigning Khanmigo to embed it directly inside practice problems, because students simply were not seeking it out on their own. This is not a story about a failed product. It is a story about the distance between a tool that works and a tool that gets used — the distance where most of the hard, human work actually lives.

What the Controlled Trials Actually Show

Here is the part that keeps me a believer. When researchers build the tutor carefully and run it as a randomized controlled trial, the results are extraordinary. At Harvard, 194 undergraduates in a physics course were split between an active learning classroom and a purpose-built AI tutor. The students with the AI tutor learned significantly more in less time — median learning gains more than double the in-class group, with an effect size the authors estimate at 0.73 to 1.3 standard deviations (Kestin et al.). That is not incremental. That is within shouting distance of Bloom's two sigma.

So the ceiling is real. A well-designed AI tutor can teach. The problem is that the Harvard result came from a meticulously prompt-engineered system in a controlled setting — not something a teenager opens at 10 p.m. on a borrowed phone. The trial proves the physics is possible. It does not prove the classroom is ready.

Why the Classroom Isn't the Lab

What breaks between the lab and the desk? Motivation, mostly. DiCerbo offered the quietest, most devastating line in the whole debate: "Students aren't great at asking questions well." A reactive tutor can only answer what a learner thinks to ask, and the students who most need help are often the ones least equipped to interrogate their own confusion. Khanmigo, by design, refuses to hand over answers and sometimes makes mistakes — virtues in a teacher, friction in a chatbot a bored fourteen-year-old can simply close.

This is the humbling truth of a human-in-the-loop system: the machine handles the explaining, but a person still has to supply the wanting. Khan's engineers built a tool that could tutor. They could not build one that could make a distracted student care. That remains, stubbornly, the teacher's job.

The Harvard trial proves the ceiling. Khanmigo shows the floor. The whole revolution lives in the space between them — and that space is made of human motivation, not better models.

The Shortage That Makes This Matter

I could treat this as an academic debate if the stakes were academic. They are not. UNESCO and the Teacher Task Force estimate the world needs 44 million additional primary and secondary teachers by 2030 to meet universal education goals — with 15 million of that shortfall concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa (UNESCO, 2024). And this is not only a supply problem in poorer nations; it is a retention crisis in richer ones. The share of primary teachers leaving the profession roughly doubled between 2015 and 2022, from 4.6% to over 9% (UNESCO, 2025). That kind of teacher attrition means the shortage is growing from both ends at once.

When you hold the RCT and the shortage in the same hand, the argument for AI stops being about test scores and becomes about arithmetic no ministry of education can solve with hiring alone. A machine that can genuinely teach — even a fraction of the time, even for the subjects that scale — is not a luxury. It is a pressure valve on a system running out of adults.

The Equity Paradox

But here is where I refuse to romanticize. The tools that get deployed today do not distribute their gains evenly. A study of teachers using Khan Academy for practice found slightly faster overall learning — but lower-performing students saw few, if any, improvements (Barnum, Chalkbeat). The benefit skewed toward students already doing well. Layer on the digital divide — the students most in need of a tutor are also the ones most likely to lack a reliable device or connection — and a tool sold as the great equalizer could quietly widen the gap it claims to close. Add the reports that a majority of U.S. teenagers now see AI-driven cheating as at least somewhat common in their schools (per a recent Pew survey cited in Chalkbeat; treat the figure cautiously), and the risk becomes concrete: the same technology can amplify a strong student or hollow out a struggling one.

UNESCO's framing, from its 2025 "Teachers cannot be coded" campaign, is the sentence I keep returning to: "While AI can support education, teachers must remain at its core, guiding students with the empathy, creativity, and judgment that no machine can replace."

What to Build Instead

So here is my honest position, from the practitioner's seat rather than the pitch deck. Stop building AI to replace the teacher, and start building it to give the teacher back their time. The winning design is not a chatbot a child must choose to open; it is a co-pilot that drafts the differentiated worksheet, flags the student who stalled on fractions three days running, and hands the human the one thing the shortage has stolen — attention to spend on the child who needs to be wanted before they can be taught.

If you build for education, build for the human-in-the-loop, not around it. Pick one teacher you actually know. Ask what task steals their evenings — grading, lesson prep, translating a note home for a parent who speaks another language. Build the smallest tool that gives that hour back, and measure whether the lowest-performing student in the room is better off, not the average. That is the test the revolution keeps failing. It is also the only one that matters. A teacher for every child was never going to be a machine. It was always going to be a machine that lets every teacher reach one more child.

Two-sigma problem
Benjamin Bloom's 1984 finding that one-on-one tutored students outperform classroom students by about two standard deviations — a gap society could never afford to close at scale, which is why AI tutoring is so tantalizing.
Randomized controlled trial
An experiment in which participants are randomly assigned to conditions, isolating the effect of the intervention. The strongest evidence for AI tutoring, like the Harvard physics study, comes from this design.
Effect size
A standardized measure of how large an intervention's impact is, expressed in standard deviations. An effect size of 0.73 to 1.3 is exceptionally large for an education intervention.
Active learning
A classroom method where students solve problems and engage directly rather than passively listen. It is a high bar to beat — which is what makes the AI tutor's win over an active learning group notable.
Human-in-the-loop
A system design in which a person remains an active decision-maker alongside the machine, rather than being replaced by it. In education, the human supplies motivation and judgment the AI cannot.
Digital divide
The gap between those with reliable access to devices and internet and those without. It threatens to route AI's benefits away from the students who need them most.
Teacher attrition
The rate at which teachers leave the profession. Rising attrition in higher-income countries turns the global teacher shortage into a retention crisis, not only a supply gap.
