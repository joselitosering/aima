For two years, the European Union told the world a flattering story about itself: that a democracy could still govern the machines it had unleashed. The AI Act was the proof — the planet's first comprehensive rulebook for artificial intelligence, adopted in August 2024 after a marathon of negotiation, with a hard date stamped on its most consequential provisions. On 2 August 2026, the obligations governing "high-risk" AI — the systems that decide who gets hired, who gets a loan, who gets flagged by police, who gets into school — would finally bite. Then, on 19 November 2025, the European Commission proposed to move that date to December 2027 (Reuters, 2025). For some uses, to August 2028. For all of them, the new trigger is not a day on the calendar at all.

A Deadline Is a Promise

Here is the thing nobody says out loud from the Berlaymont podium: a deadline is not a technicality. It is a promise made to the people a law is supposed to protect. The Commission packaged the delay inside a tidy bureaucratic parcel called the Digital Omnibus, a "simplification" bundle that also eased cookie consent and softened AI content-watermarking rules (Reuters, 2025). Simplification is one of those words that sounds like a favor until you ask who it's a favor for. The obligations being postponed are not paperwork. They are the requirement that a hiring algorithm not quietly filter out women, that a credit model not redline a neighborhood, that a biometric system used by law enforcement meet some standard of accuracy before it accuses you of a crime.

The Standards Argument, Taken Seriously

Dawn does not strawman, so let me give the delay its strongest case. The AI Act's high-risk tier only functions once the harmonized standards exist — the technical yardsticks, drafted by the European standards bodies CEN and CENELEC, that a company uses to prove it complied. Those standards were missed. Twice. They are not expected until 2026 (POLITICO Europe, 2025). Without them, a firm facing the August 2026 deadline has no agreed way to pass a conformity assessment, and many member states had not yet stood up the national authorities meant to enforce any of it. Forcing live obligations onto an unfinished scaffold would have produced legal chaos for the law-abiding and the law-breaking alike. Linking the rules' entry into force to readiness, the argument goes, is simply better lawmaking.

What the Standards Argument Hides

But the "we're only waiting for the standards" framing is exactly what makes it dangerous. A person involved in drafting those standards told Euractiv that pressing pause "could set a dangerous precedent — offering the lesson that delaying standards-drafting could be a tactic to get laws postponed or even changed" (Euractiv, 2025). Read that twice. The bottleneck being blamed for the delay is staffed and shaped by industry-influenced bodies — and those same bodies have now demonstrated that missing a standard is a lever, not a failure. This is regulatory capture in a new outfit. It doesn't need to gut the text of a law. It only needs to control the clock.

A law with no enforcement date is not a delayed law. It is an optional one.

The Transatlantic Squeeze

The pause did not arrive in a vacuum. A leaked draft of the Omnibus, reported on 7 November 2025, showed the Commission weighing exemptions — including letting companies skip registering some high-risk systems — amid what Reuters plainly described as "intense lobbying by big tech companies and criticism from the U.S. administration" (Reuters, 2025). Apple and Meta were flagged among the potential beneficiaries. There was domestic cover, too: Germany and France publicly backed a one-year pause on 18 November, with Sweden, Poland, the Czech Republic and Denmark having already called for a grace period (POLITICO Europe, 2025). So it is not a simple story of Brussels caving to Silicon Valley. It is a story of a foreign administration and a phalanx of corporations successfully softening another democracy's protections for its own citizens — and finding willing partners inside the room. Marietje Schaake, the former MEP, warned in her book The Tech Coup that we have "outsourced our democratic decision-making to a small number of people in Silicon Valley who were never elected and are not accountable to the public." She wrote it before this delay. It reads like a caption for it.

A Date That No Longer Exists

None of this is final. The Omnibus still needs approval from the Council and the European Parliament, and it could shift again (Reuters, 2025). But the mechanism is already the message. Michael McNamara, the MEP who helped negotiate the Act, warned that there should be no further delays, because more of them would undermine "regulation and rule of law beyond just the AI Act" (POLITICO Europe, 2025). He is right, and the reason is structural, not accidental. When you un-anchor a safeguard from a fixed date and re-anchor it to "whenever the standards are ready," you have handed the start date to the very industry the standards constrain. A safeguard whose beginning floats with the readiness of the regulated is a safeguard the regulated control.

So here is the discomfort to carry out of the room. The EU built the world's most ambitious AI law to prove that democratic governance of this technology was possible. The first real test of that law was not whether it could regulate the machines. It was whether it could hold a date against a year of pressure — and it moved. If the flagship can't keep its own deadline, the question is no longer whether AI can be governed. It is whether anyone with enough lobbyists ever has to wait.

The European Commission's November 2025 "simplification" package that proposed delaying the AI Act's high-risk rules and easing several digital regulations at once.
high-risk
The AI Act's tier for systems used in consequential domains — hiring, credit, education, biometrics, health, law enforcement — that carry the Act's strictest obligations.
harmonized standards
The detailed technical criteria, drafted by EU standards bodies, that companies use to demonstrate their AI systems comply with the law.
conformity assessment
The formal process by which a provider proves a high-risk AI system meets legal requirements before it is placed on the market.
entry into force
The date a legal obligation actually begins to apply; under the Omnibus it would be tied to standards readiness rather than a fixed calendar date.
regulatory capture
When the entities a rule is meant to govern gain enough influence over its design, timing, or enforcement that the rule serves them instead of the public.
