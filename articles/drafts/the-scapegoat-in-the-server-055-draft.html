In 2025, American employers announced more than 1.17 million job cuts — the highest annual total since the pandemic erased 2.2 million positions in 2020. Executives reached, again and again, for the same explanation: artificial intelligence. It sounds visionary. It sounds inevitable. It is also, on the arithmetic, mostly untrue. Of those 1.17 million cuts, the outplacement firm Challenger, Gray & Christmas could attribute roughly 55,000 to AI (Challenger, via CNBC, 2025). That is not a revolution. That is a rounding error wearing a very expensive suit.

The Number That Doesn't Add Up

Start with the ledger, because the ledger is the least sentimental thing in the room. Challenger, Gray & Christmas — the firm that has counted American layoffs for decades — tallied more than 1.17 million announced cuts in 2025. In November alone there were over 71,000, of which AI was blamed for just over 6,000 (CNBC, 2025). The individual announcements arrived with the confidence of prophecy. Amazon cut 14,000 corporate roles, its largest layoff ever, framed around investing in its "biggest bets," generative AI among them. Microsoft shed roughly 15,000 jobs across the year. Salesforce cut 4,000 customer-support workers "with the help of AI," with CEO Marc Benioff claiming the software was doing up to half the work. CrowdStrike let go 500 people — five percent of staff — and named AI outright (CNBC, 2025).

Line them up and the story frays. If AI were truly eliminating the roles, you would expect the AI-attributed share to be large and growing. Instead it is a sliver: under five percent of the year's total, in a labor market being reshaped by forces that have nothing to do with a chatbot.

The Thing Nobody Says in the Press Release

Here is the subtext the earnings call politely avoids. Companies hired frantically during the pandemic, borrowing was cheap, and then borrowing got expensive. The correction was always coming. What changed is the vocabulary. "We over-expanded and interest rates rose" is an admission of error. "AI is transforming our operating model" is a vision statement. One gets you fired; the other gets you a standing ovation. This is scapegoating in the oldest sense — loading the sins of the managers onto a convenient animal and driving it into the wilderness.

Fabian Stephany, who studies AI and work at the Oxford Internet Institute, put the mechanism plainly. He is skeptical, he told CNBC, that current layoffs reflect true efficiency gains; they read more as "a projection into AI." Rather than say "we miscalculated this two, three years ago," firms "can now come to the scapegoating, and that is saying, it's because of AI" (CNBC, 2025). Stephany also noted the plainer truth underneath: many firms significantly overhired during the pandemic, and what we are watching may be an ordinary market clearance — a correction — dressed in the season's most flattering outfit.

If the technology worked as advertised, you would not need to rehire the people it supposedly replaced.

What the Independent Data Actually Shows

When executives narrate, independent data referees. The Budget Lab at Yale went looking for AI's fingerprints in the Information sector — the corner of the economy that houses Meta, Microsoft, and their peers, and the corner where the AI-layoff story should be easiest to prove. Layoffs there did spike, reaching 66,000 in March 2026 per the government's JOLTS series. But hiring in the very same sector rose over the same window, from 2.7 percent in November 2025 to 3.4 percent by March 2026. Neither unemployment-insurance claims, nor the sector's unemployment rate, nor occupational churn corroborated an AI-driven decline in the sector's overall need for workers (Budget Lab at Yale, 2026). The Lab's own conclusion pointed elsewhere: higher borrowing costs and post-pandemic overhiring.

The one place AI's effect is measurable is not the pink slip — it is the paycheck. A difference-in-differences study across 321 occupations found that workers in high-AI-exposure jobs saw real wage growth fall about 6.7 percentage points behind their low-exposure peers after 2023, with no detectable employment effect (Impact of AI on the U.S. Labor Market, 2026). Read that again. The documented harm so far is wage compression — quieter, slower, and far less telegenic than a mass layoff. AI is squeezing what workers earn, not erasing that they exist. The narrative got the organ wrong.

Where the Automation Is Real

Honesty requires the counterweight, and I include myself in the discipline. Not every AI citation is a smokescreen. Some firms tie cuts to capability already in production, not a bet on capability that isn't. Benioff said AI was doing up to fifty percent of the support work before Salesforce cut 4,000 roles. IBM's Arvind Krishna said chatbots had absorbed the jobs of a few hundred HR staff. CrowdStrike's George Kurtz called AI a "force multiplier" — "AI flattens our hiring curve," he wrote in a securities filing, "and helps us innovate from idea to product faster" (CNBC, 2025). A force multiplier, in that usage, means fewer humans producing the same output. In these specific functions, the substitution is genuine, and dismissing every case as theater risks the opposite error.

But watch what happens next, because the tell is always in the sequel. IBM admitted it simultaneously increased hiring in critical-thinking roles — net workforce not gone, just reshuffled (CNBC, 2025). That is not a company that shed labor to a machine. That is a company moving money around and letting AI take the credit for the choreography.

The Costume, and Who Pays for It

Which brings us to the receipt. Forrester's Predictions 2026, summarized by HR Executive, found that 55 percent of employers already regret their AI-driven layoffs, and forecast that half of all AI-attributed layoffs will be quietly reversed — workers rehired, but offshore or at markedly lower salaries — often because firms cut staff betting on AI capabilities that "don't exist yet" (HR Executive, 2025). The outlet's framing was blunt: AI hype gives executives cover to make cost-cutting decisions disguised as innovation. If the machine had truly absorbed the work, no one would rehire. You would keep the savings and buy a yacht. The rehiring, the offshoring, and the missing corroboration in the independent series all point the same direction: cost-cutting wearing an AI costume.

Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson have argued that whether AI augments workers or replaces them is a corporate and political choice, not technological destiny. Kate Crawford puts it sharper: AI narratives are frequently used to launder decisions made by people. The server room did not fire anyone. Executives did — and then pointed at the server room. So here is the discomfort to carry out with you: when the refilled jobs come back at a lower wage and a longer commute across an ocean, and the press release still reads "AI transformation," who exactly did the technology serve? Not the worker. Possibly not even the shareholder. Only the story.

Scapegoating
Assigning blame for a decision to an external agent — here, AI — to avoid accountability for choices made by human managers.
Overhiring
Expanding headcount faster than sustainable demand, typically during the cheap-credit pandemic boom, making later cuts a correction rather than a technological event.
Market clearance
A labor-market correction in which excess pandemic-era hiring is unwound; Stephany's term for what many "AI layoffs" actually are.
Wage compression
The narrowing or suppression of pay growth; AI's first measurable labor effect has shown up in paychecks rather than in employment levels.
Difference-in-differences
A statistical method comparing how outcomes change for an exposed group versus a control group, used to isolate AI's effect across 321 occupations.
JOLTS
The US Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey, the federal data series tracking hires, layoffs, and openings by sector.
Force multiplier
Corporate shorthand for a tool that lets fewer workers produce the same or greater output — invoked by CrowdStrike to justify cuts.
AI-attributed layoffs
Job cuts publicly blamed on artificial intelligence, a subset of which Forrester expects to be quietly reversed.
