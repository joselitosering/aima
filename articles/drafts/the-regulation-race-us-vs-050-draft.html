I build with these tools every day. A one-person shop, a few AI agents, a pipeline that does in an afternoon what once took a room full of people a month. So when three of the most powerful governments on earth decided — within the same few weeks of summer 2025 — to write completely different rules for the technology I depend on, I paid attention. Not as a policy watcher. As someone who now has to comply with all three at once, whether I like it or not. There is no single AI rulebook in 2026. There are three, and they disagree on almost everything that matters.

Three Bets, One Technology

Here is the thing nobody planning a "global AI framework" wants to admit: the world already chose, and it chose fracture. The European Union governs AI by comprehensive, rights-first law. The United States, as of 2025, governs by deregulatory executive order and calls it an innovation strategy. China governs by fast top-down decree, exporting control as a feature. These are not three speeds on the same road. They are three roads. And the real contest — the actual race — is not who writes rules fastest. It is whose philosophy the rest of the planet ends up living under.

What made 2025 remarkable was the timing. In a single stretch of summer, the EU switched on binding obligations for general-purpose AI, the White House issued an AI Action Plan built explicitly to deregulate, and China unveiled a Global AI Governance Action Plan designed to shape everyone else's rules. Three superpowers. One technology. Three opposite bets, placed almost simultaneously.

Europe Governs by Law

The EU's answer is the most complete, and the most demanding. The AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024, and on 2 August 2025 its obligations for providers of general-purpose AI — what the law calls GPAI — began to apply, starting with transparency and copyright (European Commission). A model counts as general-purpose above a training threshold of 10^23 FLOP. The most capable ones, those crossing 10^25 FLOP, are flagged for systemic risk and carry heavier duties: notify the Commission, document, assure safety and security. Models already on the market before that August 2025 date get until 2027 to fall in line, and full enforcement by the new AI Office begins on 2 August 2026 — with power to demand documentation, evaluate models, order corrective measures, and levy fines.

The Commission frames this plainly. The rules mean, in its words, "clearer information about how AI models are trained, better enforcement of copyright protections and more responsible AI development... bringing more transparency, safety and accountability to AI systems on the market" (European Commission). This is governance as a statement of values: people first, capability second. Whatever you think of the paperwork, respect the honesty of the position.

America Governs by Executive Order

The United States pivoted the other direction, hard. "America's AI Action Plan," announced with three accompanying executive orders in July and August 2025, reorients federal policy toward innovation and deregulation — reshaping AI procurement, easing data-center permitting, and pushing American AI out to allies. The legal analysis is blunt about the intent. The plan, per Jones Day, "departs from trends seen in the European Union, the People's Republic of China, and in some state laws and regulations by prioritizing innovation, exporting American AI to allies and partners, and evaluating national security risks ahead of potential consumer protection, civil rights, privacy, and climate concerns" (Jones Day).

Read that sentence twice. It is not an accident of drafting. It is a ranking. National security and speed ahead of consumer protection, civil rights, privacy, and climate. As of mid-2026 there is still no comprehensive federal AI statute in the US — a point worth flagging as needing live re-verification against the current Congressional record — which means the executive branch, not the legislature, is setting the tempo. That is a fragile foundation. Executive orders can be revoked with the next signature, and the previous administration's 2023 AI safety order was.

Three superpowers, one technology, three opposite bets — and every builder now has to comply with all of them at once.

China Governs by Decree

China moved fastest of all, and most deliberately. At the World Artificial Intelligence Conference on 26 July 2025, it issued a Global AI Governance Action Plan — a bid to write international rules on its own terms — alongside draft AI ethics measures (Mayer Brown). Its "AI Plus" guideline set penetration targets for new-generation intelligent terminals and AI agents exceeding 70% by 2027 and 90% by 2030. And on 1 September 2025, its Measures for Labelling of AI-Generated Synthetic Content took effect with the national standard GB 45438-2025, requiring both explicit labels a person can see and implicit labels embedded in file metadata, and obliging platforms to verify and flag suspected AI content (Mayer Brown). It is the most prescriptive content-labelling regime of the three — control exported as a governance product.

The Brussels Effect Meets Its Limit

The optimists have a rebuttal, and it deserves a fair hearing. Divergence, they argue, is a feature. Let each bloc pick its trade-off — EU rights, US speed, China control — and the strictest regime becomes a de facto global floor anyway. This is the Brussels effect: because a global company would rather build one compliant product than three, everyone ends up meeting the toughest bar, which is Europe's. Convergence through the market, no treaty required.

I wanted to believe that. But 2025 cut against it. The US did not quietly settle onto the European floor — it issued an Action Plan engineered to diverge and to export a rival, deregulatory model to its allies. China built its own global-governance plan to export a third. When the two largest AI powers actively campaign to spread incompatible norms, "convergence via the strictest regime" stops being automatic. Fragmentation becomes the baseline. And there is a deeper worry that cuts across all three: as former MEP Marietje Schaake has argued, we have in effect "outsourced our democratic decision-making to a small number of people in Silicon Valley who were never elected and are not accountable to the public" (Schaake, quotation to be verified before print). Three regulators, and the code still gets written in the same handful of buildings.

What This Means for the Rest of Us

Regulation feels like something that happens above us, to us. It isn't. It happens through us — through the tools we choose and the way we use them. The pacing problem is real: laws move on multi-year cycles while models turn over in months, so no framework will ever be perfectly current. That is not a reason to wait for clarity that will never come. It is a reason to build with intention now.

So here is the concrete first step. Before your next AI project ships, write down three things: what data trained the model you are using, whether your outputs are labelled as AI-made, and which of the three regimes your audience lives under. That is not lawyering. That is the practitioner's version of citizenship. The rules are being written whether or not we show up — and the builders who understand all three philosophies are the ones who will help decide which one wins. Show up.
