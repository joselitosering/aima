THE PERSUASION ENGINE: AI, SOCIAL MEDIA, AND THE DEATH OF SHARED REALITY

JS
Joselito Sering
Editor-in-Chief · AIMA
Published
July 4, 2026
Read Time
9 min read
Category
AI Ethics

In 2018, Facebook's engineers were handed a mandate to increase "meaningful social interactions." They optimized hard, tuned the algorithm carefully, and delivered exactly what the business model required. What they got — what they built, whether they fully understood it or not — was an outrage machine. And the reason it worked is the same reason the AI-amplified version works today: outrage is the highest-engagement emotion, and engagement is all the model was ever asked to optimize for.

THE ARCHITECTURE OF OUTRAGE

The Facebook memo leaked in 2021, but the decision it documented had been made three years earlier. Frances Haugen, a product manager who worked at the company until her departure and subsequent whistleblowing, handed the Wall Street Journal a cache of internal research that established something the public had long suspected: the platform's algorithm had been deliberately tuned to reward content that provoked strong emotional reactions, and the strongest reactions it reliably surfaced were anger, tribal loyalty, and fear (Haugen / CBS News, 2021).

The internal research was unambiguous. Major European political parties told Facebook researchers that the 2018 algorithm changes had forced them to alter their communications strategies — that moderation and nuance got buried while incendiary messaging got reach. The algorithm didn't care about the truth of what it amplified. It cared about the engagement metric. The outrage machine was not a glitch. It was the feature.

Shoshana Zuboff named the underlying logic of surveillance capitalism before many people were ready to hear it. "It's a marketplace that trades exclusively in human futures," she wrote in The Age of Surveillance Capitalism (2019). Social media platforms are not in the business of connecting people. They are in the business of predicting and modifying human behavior — and selling those predictions to advertisers, political campaigns, and anyone else willing to pay for access to a captive, conditioned audience. Attention is the commodity. Outrage, fear, and tribal confirmation are the highest-yield sources of that commodity. The architecture has no conscience because it was never designed to need one.

The decision to optimize for engagement above accuracy — above health, above social cohesion, above shared reality — was made in boardrooms and engineering meetings before a single convincing synthetic image existed. What AI adds to this story is not a new moral failure. It is the industrialization of an existing one.

⚠️
The Outrage Engine — By Design
Facebook's 2018 "Meaningful Social Interactions" algorithm change is documented in the Facebook Files — internal research leaked by whistleblower Frances Haugen. The internal data showed that the update, intended to prioritize content that "brought people together," instead systematically rewarded divisive, emotionally provocative posts. Major European political parties internally reported that the algorithm forced them to skew their messaging toward more extreme, more negative content to maintain reach. This predates generative AI by years.

ENTER THE PERSUASION ENGINE

Here is what changes when you add AI to an architecture already optimized for engagement: scale, precision, and plausibility. All three together, simultaneously, at a cost that approaches zero.

In 2023, researchers estimated roughly 500,000 deepfakes existed across the online information ecosystem. By 2025, cybersecurity firm DeepStrike placed that figure at approximately 8 million — annual growth nearing 900% (DeepStrike / Fortune, 2025). Voice cloning has crossed what Siwei Lyu, Director of the Media Forensic Lab at the University at Buffalo, calls "the indistinguishable threshold": a few seconds of audio now suffice to generate a convincing voice clone, complete with natural intonation, rhythm, and breathing. Some major retailers report receiving over a thousand AI-generated scam calls per day. Lyu predicts 2026 will see entire video-call participants synthesized in real time — interactive AI actors whose faces, voices, and mannerisms adapt instantly to a prompt. This is not science fiction. This is the current development trajectory.

The volume is alarming, but the virality finding is more alarming still. Researchers analyzing 91,452 misleading posts flagged through X's Community Notes platform found that AI-generated misinformation is significantly more likely to go viral than human-generated misinformation — despite typically originating from smaller accounts with fewer followers (Drolsbach & Pröllochs, arXiv:2505.10266, 2025). AI-generated misinformation tends toward more positive sentiment, emphasizes entertaining or shareable framing, and achieves disproportionate spread precisely because it doesn't trigger the skepticism readers bring to obviously partisan or angry content. It looks reasonable. It reads well. It doesn't announce itself as propaganda.

A large-scale experimental study published in PNAS Nexus in 2026 reinforced this. Across 27 European countries — 27,227 respondents, eight news items per participant — people were marginally more likely to perceive AI-generated news as real and to indicate willingness to share it. AI-generated content was rated slightly more credible than human-generated content (2.40 versus 2.34 on a four-point scale). The researchers concluded that the data "reinforces the pattern that AI-generated content can produce more compelling and persuasive disinformation" (Stefkovics & Gere, PNAS Nexus, Vol. 5, Issue 3, 2026). More credible. More shareable. Produced at effectively zero marginal cost.

Renée DiResta, whose research at the Stanford Internet Observatory has documented how influence operations exploit the architecture of social platforms, names the operational reality plainly: "The platforms make it possible to spread manipulative narratives with phenomenal ease, and without very much money." What AI adds is the capacity to make those narratives individually tailored. The broadcast propaganda model — one message to millions — is giving way to the personalized persuasion model: millions of slightly different messages, each architectured to the individual psychological profile of the recipient. Not propaganda. Precision behavioral modification.

The outrage machine was not a glitch. It was the feature. AI didn't create it — it put it on an assembly line.

— Joselito Sering, AIMA

THE LIAR'S DIVIDEND

Here is the most counterintuitive part of this story, and the part that gets the least attention: deepfakes cause epistemic damage even when they're never used.

Legal scholars Bobby Chesney (University of Texas) and Danielle Citron (University of Virginia) coined the term "liar's dividend" in a 2019 paper to describe the following mechanism. Once the public knows that synthetic media exists — that convincing video and audio of anyone saying or doing anything can be fabricated — authentic footage becomes permanently deniable. A politician doesn't need to produce a single fake video. The existence of fake videos in the information ecosystem does the work for them. When a real recording surfaces, they simply claim it's AI-generated. The burden of proof has been reversed. Evidence must now prove its own authenticity, and that proof is increasingly difficult to produce convincingly to a general audience (Chesney & Citron / CSET Georgetown, 2019).

The dividend isn't paid in fake videos. It's paid in doubt.

The practical materializations have already arrived. In the 2024 New Hampshire Democratic primary, a robocall using an AI-synthesized voice impersonating President Biden was used to suppress Democratic voter turnout — the voice urging recipients not to vote, leveraging the President's own vocal patterns against the democratic process. The Federal Election Commission had opened a rulemaking process on AI in political advertising in 2023; no final rule was in place before the November 2024 election. Section 230, a law written for dial-up internet in 1996, remained the primary US content moderation framework governing AI-generated political content on platforms.

In Ireland in 2025, a fabricated video showed the eventual presidential election winner announcing his withdrawal — complete with synthetic national broadcaster graphics and fake news anchor footage, released days before polling. Researchers at the Centre for Emerging Technology and Security documented it as the first deepfake specifically designed to suppress votes by fabricating a candidate withdrawal. The candidate won. But the incident deposited something in the public record that no correction can fully undo: the knowledge that such a video could be real, could be watched and believed, and could move votes before anyone had a chance to verify it (CETAS / Turing Institute, 2025).

The asymmetry is the point. Bad actors profit from the liar's dividend without doing anything. They don't need a deepfake laboratory. They need the public to know deepfakes exist — which is now universal knowledge — and they need authentic documentation of their actions to arrive in an environment already saturated with plausible deniability. The architecture provides both conditions for free.

⚠️
The Liar's Dividend — How It Works
The mechanism is asymmetric: once synthetic media is publicly known to exist, it permanently burdens authentic evidence with the requirement to prove its own reality. Bad actors who produce no deepfakes benefit from the existence of deepfakes made by others. Real footage of genuine actions can be dismissed as fabricated. The weapon does not need to be fired to cause damage — its existence on the battlefield changes the rules of engagement for everyone.

THE DEATH OF SHARED REALITY

The academic literature has begun to name what we are actually watching, and the names matter because they carry precise meaning that vague terms like "polarization" or "misinformation" obscure. The filter bubble, as described by Eli Pariser in 2011, is about algorithmic narrowing — the gradual restriction of information exposure as recommendation systems learn preferences and optimize for engagement. Evidence for filter bubbles is real and documented. But what emerges after years of this process, at scale, with generative AI accelerating content production, is something more severe: epistemic fragmentation.

Epistemic fragmentation is not narrowing. It is incompatibility. Different communities don't merely have different opinions about the same facts — they inhabit different information ecosystems so fully segregated that they no longer share a common factual baseline at all. They experience different realities. The concern is not ideological division — societies have always had that. The concern is that democratic deliberation requires a shared factual foundation on which citizens can disagree about values and policy. Without that foundation, politics becomes a contest between competing fictions rather than competing interpretations of shared facts (Loth, Kappes & Pahl, arXiv:2602.02100, 2026).

The researchers identify something they call epistemic learned helplessness as the primary long-term threat. Not that people come to believe specific lies — that is a recoverable condition. The deeper threat is that people stop believing the question of truth is worth asking. Verification feels futile. Survey data cited in World Economic Forum analysis reports that roughly 70% of respondents struggle to trust online information because they cannot tell if AI generated it, and approximately 64% fear elections are being manipulated by AI content (WEF, 2026; figures sourced from search excerpts, primary methodology unconfirmed). Europol has separately projected that up to 90% of online content may be synthetically generated by 2026 — a projection, not a measurement, but the direction of travel is not in dispute.

This is the condition the persuasion engine produces at scale: not a more deceived population, but a population that has abandoned the practice of being undeceived. A population for whom the question "Is this real?" has become so exhausting that it goes unasked. That is not a world with more lies in it. It is a world where the concept of shared factual truth has lost its operational meaning.

THE COUNTERARGUMENT THAT MAKES IT WORSE

There is a legitimate challenge to the strongest version of this argument, and it deserves a direct answer rather than a dismissal, because engaging with it precisely is what separates a structural critique from a moral panic.

A 2024 naturalistic experiment published in PNAS, involving nearly 9,000 YouTube participants, found that deliberately manipulating recommendations to create filter-bubble conditions had limited effects on measured political polarization. A systematic review of peer-reviewed studies from 2015–2025 found that while algorithms structurally amplify ideological homogeneity, users show partial awareness of algorithmic feeds and exercise some navigational agency. The claim that social media wholesale "radicalized" entire populations may be empirically overstated — correlation between platform use and polarization does not establish the causal direction, and the strongest versions of the radicalization-pipeline story have not survived careful experimental scrutiny (PNAS, 2024; Social Sciences, MDPI, 2025).

Take this seriously. The counterevidence is real.

But it addresses the wrong threat. The PNAS study measured short-term polarization effects from filter-bubble manipulation, in a pre-generative-AI information environment. It focused on the radicalization mechanism — the claim that algorithms push users toward extreme positions. That is a meaningful and empirically contestable claim. The stronger claim, and the one with accumulating high-confidence evidence, is different.

AI-authored misinformation goes viral faster than human-authored misinformation, regardless of the audience's existing political position. AI-generated content is rated marginally more credible across a 27-country sample of 27,000 people. Deepfakes have already been used in presidential elections on two continents. And the liar's dividend operates without any individual act of deception — the mere existence of convincing synthetic media permanently changes the epistemic weight of authentic documentation. These are distinct mechanisms with distinct evidence bases, and the counterevidence that complicates the radicalization story does not touch them.

Research Signal — arXiv:2505.10266 (2025)
Analysis of 91,452 misleading posts flagged through X's Community Notes platform found that AI-generated misinformation achieves disproportionate viral spread compared to human-generated misinformation — despite originating from accounts with fewer followers. AI-generated misleading content shows more positive sentiment, prioritizes entertainment framing, and bypasses traditional credibility filters. Source: Drolsbach & Pröllochs, arXiv:2505.10266, 2025.

The question for 2026 is not "Do algorithms push people toward extremes?" It is: what happens to the shared epistemic foundation of democratic life when AI-authored misinformation systematically outperforms human-authored information in reach and credibility, when the indistinguishable threshold for synthetic media has already been crossed, and when the architecture that distributes all of this was designed from the start to optimize for engagement over accuracy?

Disinformation researchers surveyed in 2026 framed the primary risk not as a world where people believe a specific lie, but as one where they stop believing in the possibility of truth altogether — epistemic learned helplessness at civilizational scale. A byproduct of an architecture that was always, at its core, in the business of manufacturing doubt as a precondition for selling certainty. The architecture of attention is optimized for engagement. Engagement rewards outrage. Outrage corrodes the shared reality on which democratic deliberation depends. The persuasion engine is not failing. It is succeeding. The question is who profits from the world it is building, and whether we have constructed any institution with enough independence to make that question matter.

Glossary

Liar's Dividend ↑ Back to text

A concept coined by legal scholars Bobby Chesney and Danielle Citron in 2019. It describes the phenomenon by which the public existence of convincing synthetic media — deepfakes — allows bad actors to plausibly deny authentic footage or recordings as AI-generated. The "dividend" accrues to liars: even genuine evidence of real actions loses its evidentiary weight once the public understands that fabrication is possible and indistinguishable. Crucially, bad actors do not need to produce any fake content — the existence of fake content made by others does the work for them.

Epistemic Fragmentation ↑ Back to text

A condition in which different social groups inhabit such fully segregated information ecosystems that they no longer share a common factual baseline — not merely different opinions, but incompatible realities. Distinct from the "filter bubble" concept, which implies gradual narrowing within a shared information environment. Fragmentation implies full incompatibility: citizens who cannot agree on what happened, not just on what it means. Democratic deliberation requires a shared factual foundation; epistemic fragmentation dissolves that foundation.

Epistemic Learned Helplessness ↑ Back to text

A condition, identified in disinformation research literature, in which people abandon truth-seeking behavior not because they are deceived about specific facts, but because the act of verification has become so exhausting and unrewarding that they stop attempting it. The danger is not that people believe false things — that is recoverable. The danger is that they stop caring whether things are true. Named by analogy to psychological "learned helplessness," in which repeated exposure to uncontrollable negative outcomes leads organisms to stop attempting to escape them even when escape becomes possible.

Filter Bubble ↑ Back to text

A term coined by internet activist Eli Pariser in 2011 to describe the way algorithmic content curation gradually narrows users' information exposure based on inferred preferences — creating a "bubble" of ideologically compatible content. Evidence for filter bubbles is empirically documented. It differs from epistemic fragmentation in degree and reversibility: filter bubbles narrow exposure within a shared information ecosystem; fragmentation implies incompatible realities with no shared baseline remaining. Research has found that filter-bubble effects on political polarization are real but often overstated in popular accounts.

Surveillance Capitalism ↑ Back to text

A term coined by Harvard Business School professor Shoshana Zuboff in her 2019 book of the same name. It describes the economic logic of large technology platforms: behavioral data extracted from users at scale is used to build predictive models of human action, which are then sold to advertisers and other parties seeking to modify behavior. Under surveillance capitalism, the product is not the service — the product is the user's predicted future behavior. Social media platforms are the primary infrastructure; AI dramatically expands the precision and personalization of behavioral modification at scale.

AI-Generated Misinformation ↑ Back to text

False or misleading content produced using artificial intelligence tools — including large language models, image generators, voice cloning systems, and video synthesis. Distinct from human-authored misinformation in several operationally important ways: it can be produced at near-zero marginal cost, it tends to exhibit more neutral or positive framing that bypasses credibility heuristics, and peer-reviewed research has found it achieves disproportionate viral spread compared to equivalent human-authored content. The term covers a spectrum from fully fabricated claims to subtly misleading framing generated at scale.
