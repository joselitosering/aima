AI Ethics

Your AI Ethics Board
Is a Press Release

DG
Dawn Ginhaua
Cultural Critic & Educator · AIMA
Published
June 19, 2026
Read Time
8 min read
Category
AI Ethics

In 2019, Google’s AI ethics board lasted eleven days before being dissolved without producing a single document. In 2023, Microsoft laid off its entire AI ethics and society team — approximately seven people — during a round of cuts framed as reducing costs, announced one week before the company pledged to deploy AI more aggressively. Meta’s Oversight Board has reviewed hundreds of individual content decisions. It has never reviewed the algorithmic feed that determines what three billion people see. After more than a decade of high-profile ethics initiatives at the world’s largest AI companies, not one published ethics board has ever disclosed blocking a product from shipping. Not one. This is not governance failure. It is governance functioning as designed.

What Nobody Calls It

Corporate AI ethics boards are not accountability mechanisms. They are demand management tools — designed to absorb criticism, signal seriousness, and protect the core business from policy interference. The clearest evidence for this claim is not hidden. It is in the founding charters, the resignation letters, and the legal structures of the institutions themselves.

What is remarkable is how rarely anyone says it plainly. The word “ethics” carries such cultural prestige that attaching it to a committee is enough to shift the conversational burden onto critics, who must now argue against something that has already claimed the moral high ground. You are not arguing that the company is doing wrong — you are arguing against their ethics board. The rhetorical architecture is elegant.

📋
On Definitions
An ethics board that cannot say no is not an ethics board. It is a press release with a panel. The distinction matters because the two things require different responses. Governance failures call for reform. Governance theater calls for a different conversation entirely — one about why the theater exists and who it serves.

The most productive way to read corporate AI ethics boards is not as sincere but ineffective institutions — well-meaning structures that simply lack enforcement power. It is to read them as rational institutions, ones that are functioning correctly according to the actual goals they were built to achieve. Those goals are: manage activist pressure, prevent binding external regulation, and maintain the appearance of self-governance while keeping product decisions internal.

When you read them that way, they are not failing. They are succeeding.

The Paper Trail

The record, assembled without editorializing:

Google’s Advanced Technology External Advisory Council, 2019. Announced March 26. Dissolved April 4. Eight days. The board produced no work product. No principles. No recommendations. No minutes. It collapsed after employee protests over two members: a Heritage Foundation fellow who had publicly opposed trans rights legislation, and a drone-warfare executive. What the board could not survive was the first internal disagreement about membership. It did not reach the stage where it might have faced a disagreement about products. Within two years, Google had signed a $1.2 billion contract with the Israeli government to provide cloud and AI infrastructure that would later be confirmed as operational in a live military conflict.

Microsoft’s AI Ethics and Society team, 2023. Dissolved in January as part of a 10,000-person layoff. No successor function was publicly announced. One week later, Microsoft announced the integration of GPT-4 into Bing and a major acceleration of AI product deployment across its platform. The responsible AI principles page remained live throughout, unchanged. Ethics, as a funded organizational function, had been reclassified as an efficiency target.

Meta’s Oversight Board, 2020–present. Structurally, the most serious attempt in the industry: independent membership, a $130 million endowment, published decisions with binding effect on content moderation cases. Its jurisdiction covers individual content appeals. The board can rule on whether a specific post should be reinstated. It cannot rule on whether the recommendation algorithm that decides which posts three billion people see should exist in its current form. The board has no authority over product design, training data, or the feed mechanics that have been linked in academic literature to the amplification of political radicalization and eating disorders in adolescents. Those systems are outside its remit, by design, and were designed to be.

Amazon’s facial recognition moratorium, 2020. Amazon announced in June 2020 that it would halt sales of its Rekognition facial recognition system to law enforcement for one year, to give Congress time to enact appropriate legislation. Congress did not enact legislation. Amazon’s stated position on what would happen at the end of the year was never formally clarified. The moratorium expired without a public announcement. Whether the sales resumed is not something Amazon has disclosed.

IBM’s facial recognition exit, 2020. IBM CEO Arvind Krishna published an open letter to Congress announcing that the company was exiting the general-purpose facial recognition business due to concerns about racial justice and civil liberties. IBM’s facial recognition market share was small. The announcement was widely covered as a principled stand. The open letter did not address IBM’s law enforcement analytics contracts, which deployed related pattern-recognition capabilities under different product names. Those contracts were not discussed.

⚖️
The Exception That Proves the Pattern
The EU AI Act — which became law in 2024 — does something that not one of these ethics boards has done: it creates binding obligations. High-risk AI systems require conformity assessments, technical documentation, and human oversight mechanisms with penalties of up to 6% of global annual revenue. The EU AI Act did not emerge from a corporate ethics board. It emerged from a legislative process that included, at various points, sustained lobbying efforts by the same companies whose ethics boards are described above. The presence of binding law required binding opposition to binding law. The ethics boards were notably absent from that particular fight.

The Architecture

The pattern is not coincidental. Ethics theater follows a consistent structure, and that consistency is itself evidence of design rather than drift.

Phase one: An activist campaign, investigative report, or regulatory threat creates reputational pressure. The pressure is specific — a particular product, a particular use case, a particular group of workers or communities who have been harmed by something the company built.

Phase two: A board, committee, or responsible AI initiative is announced. It is staffed with credentialed academics and civil society advocates. Their professional affiliation — their university, their prior advocacy work, their published research on algorithmic fairness — provides reputational cover for the company. Their formal authority within corporate governance is advisory only. They can recommend. They cannot block.

Phase three: The board operates, usually through a period of principle-writing. Principles are documents that describe what a company values, not what it has committed not to do. “We are committed to fairness, transparency, and human oversight” does not mean “we will not deploy this system before completing an independent audit.” The gap between those two statements is where the harm lives, and where the document carefully does not go.

Phase four: The first moment of real constraint — when the board produces something that would require the company to not do something — is also the last. Either the board is dissolved; or the recommendation is noted, considered, and filed; or the academics who raised the concern resign quietly and the board continues with remaining members. The company proceeds. The page of principles remains live.

“The difference between ethics and ethics theater is whether the word ‘no’ appears in the governance documentation — and who is required to obey it.”
— Dawn Ginhaua

Hannah Arendt described the bureaucratic diffusion of moral responsibility as a structure that allows ordinary institutions to participate in extraordinary harm while remaining, at each individual decision point, technically not accountable. No single actor approves the harm. Everyone is consulted. No one is responsible. The AI ethics board, at its current level of institutional power, is precisely such a structure. Responsibility is distributed across a roster of impressive names, each of whom was consulted, none of whom had binding authority, and none of whom can afterward be said to have approved what was built. The structure does not prevent harm. It provides a documented process to cite after the fact.

This is not a story about individual bad actors. Most of the academics who have served on these boards are serious researchers with genuine commitments to the issues they were recruited to address. The structure renders those commitments inert. Moral seriousness, without institutional power, is a character reference. It is not governance.

What Accountability Looks Like

There are counterexamples, and they share a feature the ethics boards do not: the governance structure can produce a consequence that the business is required to absorb.

The EU AI Act creates binding legal obligations backed by financial penalties. Signal’s refusal to participate in advertising data markets is a design constraint embedded in the product architecture, not a stakeholder recommendation. Anthropic’s Constitutional AI embeds behavioral rules into the training process itself, producing auditable outputs rather than advisory documents. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework, while voluntary, is referenced in federal procurement contracts — giving it material consequences that a principles document does not have. None of these mechanisms are perfect. All of them require the business to do something it might not otherwise choose to do. That is what distinguishes a governance structure from a governance document.

The relevant question is not whether a company is sincere about ethics. Several of the academics who have served on corporate boards are among the most rigorous AI safety and fairness researchers working today. The question is whether the governance structure gives their seriousness any operational consequence. An ethics board staffed with genuinely serious people, given advisory authority, produces principled advice that companies are free to ignore. The record shows that, on the questions that matter, this is what they do.

David Graeber argued that bureaucratic structures often survive not because they accomplish their stated purpose, but because they produce the appearance of purposeful activity. The ethics board meeting is held. The principles document is circulated. The launch proceeds. Nothing changed, but everyone has participated in something that looked like due diligence. The paperwork is complete. The harm is downstream.

The Question

Corporate AI ethics boards will continue to be announced. The press releases will continue to name prestigious academics. LinkedIn congratulations will accumulate. Trade publications will run respectful profiles. The boards will publish principles. The products will ship.

None of this is concealed. The charter documents are available. The legal structures are on file. The resignation letters are archived. The gap between what these institutions are described as — accountability structures, governance mechanisms, ethical guardrails — and what they are authorized to do is not a secret. It is simply not something that corporate communications departments find useful to foreground, and not something that most coverage of these institutions pauses to examine.

So here is the question that does not appear on the agenda at the panel discussion marking the launch, or in the announcement, or in the trade publication profile, or in the LinkedIn post celebrating the board’s formation:

If your ethics board has never stopped a product from shipping — not once, not one deployment, not one rollout paused or delayed or redesigned as a direct result of the board’s recommendation — what exactly is it for?

And who decided that was sufficient?
