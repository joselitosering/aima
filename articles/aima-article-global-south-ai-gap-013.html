The Global South AI Gap: Who Gets Left Behind When the Future Arrives

JS
Joselito Sering
Editor-in-Chief · AIMA
Published
June 18, 2026
Read Time
12 min read
Category
AI Society

Somewhere in northern Nigeria, a nursing student opens an AI study assistant on her phone. The tool has been used by more than 500 million people and speaks 97 languages. When she types in Hausa — the language she thinks in, the language she will one day use to explain diagnoses to patients — the system stumbles, guesses, and misses the point. There are over 2,000 languages spoken across Africa. Most of them, to the world's most powerful AI systems, simply do not exist. What we call the AI revolution is, for roughly half of humanity, someone else's revolution.

The Geometry of Exclusion

Think of the digital divide as a map drawn not in countries but in percentages. In the first quarter of 2026, 27.5 percent of the population in the Global North was regularly using generative AI tools. In the Global South — the nations of Africa, Latin America, South and Southeast Asia, and the Pacific — that number was 15.4 percent. The gap seems small until you notice the direction of travel: adoption is growing more than twice as fast in higher-income regions. The distance between those two numbers is not narrowing. It is widening.

The International Monetary Fund has developed an AI Readiness Index that tries to capture a country's ability to benefit from artificial intelligence — measuring infrastructure, human capital, regulatory environment, and integration into the digital economy. Advanced economies score 0.68 on average. Emerging markets score 0.46. Low-income countries score 0.32. That last number is not simply lower than the first. It is less than half. The IMF is not measuring who has the better chatbot. It is measuring who has the foundational conditions to participate in the economy that AI is now creating.

This is the geometry of exclusion. Not a wall, but a slope — and the people starting at the bottom are expected to run uphill while carrying everything they already lack.

📊
IMF AI Readiness Index — 2026
Advanced economies average 0.68 · Emerging markets average 0.46 · Low-income countries average 0.32. The gap between the top and bottom tiers is not closing — it reflects decades of compounding underinvestment in electricity, connectivity, digital skills, and local data infrastructure. A country that cannot reliably power its hospitals cannot train its population on AI tools. These deficits are linked, not separate.

When the Servers Aren't There

Every AI system runs on hardware — computing infrastructure housed in enormous, climate-controlled data centers that require vast amounts of stable electricity, fast fiber connections, and continuous cooling. This is not glamorous infrastructure. It is the invisible plumbing of the intelligence revolution. And right now, it is almost entirely located in one place.

Africa is home to 18 percent of the world's population. It holds less than 1 percent of global data center capacity. As of September 2025, approximately 23 gigawatts of new data center capacity was under construction globally. Roughly 75 percent of that was being built in the United States. The servers deciding humanity's future sit in rooms where the temperature never rises above 68 degrees and the power never goes out — a description that applies to perhaps four percent of the planet.

The per-capita computing disparity is even starker. The United States has approximately 20,000 times more servers per capita than low-income countries, and 200 times more than middle-income countries. This is not a modest advantage. It is a different universe of possibility. An AI model trained on American servers, with American electricity, on data collected primarily from American and European users, reflects the world that built it — and quietly ignores the world that didn't.

⚠️
The Compute Gap
The United States has 20,000× more servers per capita than low-income countries. Africa holds less than 1% of global data center capacity despite housing 18% of the world's population. Less than 5% of populations in low-income countries possess basic digital skills, compared to 66% in high-income countries. The infrastructure problem and the skills problem compound each other: there is little incentive to build digital skills when there is no reliable infrastructure to use them on.

The skills gap mirrors the infrastructure gap. Fewer than 5 percent of people in low-income countries possess basic digital skills, compared to 66 percent in high-income nations. It is tempting to frame this as a failure of education. It is more accurately a failure of investment — because digital skills develop where there is something to practice them on, and 2.6 billion people worldwide still lack reliable internet access. You cannot learn to swim in a country with no water.

The Language of Power

Language is not just communication. It is the container of thought — the medium through which a person understands the world, forms conclusions, seeks help, and passes knowledge to the next generation. An AI system that does not speak your language does not merely inconvenience you. It tells you, in the most fundamental way, that you were not considered.

The major large language models — the conversational AI systems now being integrated into healthcare, education, legal aid, and commerce worldwide — were built overwhelmingly on English-language data, reflecting North American and European social and cultural norms. When researchers at Stanford published their 2025 AI Index, the performance disparity between English and every other language class was one of the report's most consistent findings. The systems are not equally capable in all languages. They are dramatically better in the language of the people who built them.

In 2025, researchers published the SAHARA benchmark — the most comprehensive evaluation of AI performance across African languages ever conducted. They tested 517 languages across 16 standard NLP tasks. The findings confirmed what African linguists had been arguing for years: a pronounced and consistent performance gap persists between English and the vast majority of African languages, including widely spoken ones such as Hausa, Wolof, Oromo, and Kinyarwanda. More than 2,000 languages spoken on the African continent have remained on the sidelines, trapped in what researchers called "a cycle of being labeled low-resource" — a category that describes not linguistic complexity but decades of deliberate underinvestment in digital data collection.

🔬
The SAHARA Benchmark — 2025
A 2025 study evaluated AI performance across 517 African languages across 16 NLP tasks. Result: a pronounced, consistent performance gap between English and virtually every African language — including major ones spoken by tens of millions of people. The root cause is not linguistic difficulty but policy-driven data inequity: decades of failing to collect, digitize, and make available the text and speech data that AI systems need to learn. The languages weren't low-resource. They were low-priority.

We built a mind that speaks 97 languages and then called the other 6,900 low-resource.

— AIMA Editorial

There are nascent efforts to correct this. The African Next Voices project, announced in 2025, recorded and transcribed 9,000 hours of everyday conversation across dozens of African languages. A team of African researchers, supported by a $2.2 million Gates Foundation grant, created AI-ready open-access datasets representative of real African speech patterns and cultural contexts. These efforts are meaningful. They are also profoundly underfunded relative to the scale of the problem — and the speed at which the dominant systems are calcifying.

Where the Money Doesn't Go

In 2024, private sector investment in AI in the United States reached $109.1 billion. Approximately 40 percent of all corporate AI research and development spending globally is concentrated in roughly 100 firms — almost all of them located in the United States or China. These are not just commercial facts. They are gravitational facts. Capital flows toward existing capital. Infrastructure gets built where infrastructure already exists. Data is collected where there are already enough devices to generate it.

Africa received 0.02 percent of global AI funding in the second quarter of 2024. Not 2 percent. Not 0.2 percent. Zero point zero two. The continent that is home to a fifth of humanity, that holds some of the world's youngest and fastest-growing populations, that will be central to every major demographic and economic story of the 21st century — received one five-thousandth of the money being directed at what many credible analysts consider the most transformative technology in human history.

⚡
The Investment Chasm
US private AI investment in 2024: $109.1 billion. Africa's share of global AI funding (Q2 2024): 0.02%. Africa's share of projected global AI investment to 2030: 0.3%. Projected AI economic benefit for Latin America: 3% of global total. Yet AI could add $2.9 trillion to Africa's economy by 2030 alone — if the investment actually arrives. UNCTAD estimates the total global AI economic opportunity at $4.8 trillion. The regions being left out of the investment are the ones that would benefit most from inclusion.

Latin America's situation is only marginally better. If current development trajectories hold, just 3 percent of the projected global economic benefits from AI will flow to Latin American nations. The UNDP has warned explicitly that artificial intelligence, left unmanaged, risks sparking "a new era of divergence" — reversing decades of progress toward narrowing development gaps between nations. Reversing. Not merely slowing.

The math is almost philosophical in its perversity: the regions that could benefit most from AI-assisted leaps in education, healthcare, agriculture, and public administration are receiving the least investment in the infrastructure that would make those leaps possible.

The View from Kampala

None of this is inevitable. That is the important thing to hold onto — not as comfort, but as accountability. The AI gap is not a law of physics. It is the aggregate result of decisions made by governments, corporations, multilateral institutions, and the individual engineers and executives who choose what to build, for whom, and at what price.

From Kampala to Accra to Nairobi, a different kind of AI story is beginning to emerge. African engineers are building language models for African languages. African healthcare researchers are adapting diagnostic AI for diseases and patient populations that Western training data largely ignores. Kenyan startups are building agriculture tools trained on African soil data. Nigerian fintech companies are using AI to extend financial services to populations that traditional banks have never served. These efforts are real. They are growing. And they are still, by almost any measure, inadequately supported.

✓
What Good AI for the Global South Looks Like
The African Next Voices Project (2025): 9,000 hours of recorded, transcribed everyday conversation across dozens of African languages — now open access for AI training. The Gates Foundation's $2.2M dataset initiative: AI-ready corpora representing real African speech and cultural context. UNCTAD's AI framework for developing nations: emphasizes local data ownership, open-access compute, and regulatory capacity-building as prerequisites — not afterthoughts. CSIS analysis (2026): identifies NGO-partnered AI deployments in healthcare and agriculture as among the highest-impact applications available to low-income nations right now.

The infrastructure gap is not unbridgeable. UNCTAD estimates Africa would need approximately $2.6 trillion in infrastructure investment by 2030 to bring its digital capacity to a level where AI tools can be meaningfully deployed at scale. That number sounds enormous until you compare it to the $109 billion the United States invested in AI in a single year, or the $4.8 trillion in global economic value that AI is projected to generate. The math of inclusion is not more expensive than the math of exclusion. It is simply less convenient.

The Choice Ahead

There is a version of the AI future in which the low-resource language problem gets quietly solved because the market eventually expands. In which African data centers get built because Chinese or American companies see profit in serving African cloud demand. In which the AI readiness gap closes because cheap smartphones and satellite internet make AI tools accessible everywhere. This version exists. It is possible. It also takes decades, and the systems being deployed right now — the medical diagnosis tools, the loan approval algorithms, the content moderation systems, the educational platforms — will operate on populations worldwide long before those decades have passed.

The AI systems being built today will encode whatever values, priorities, and assumptions existed during their construction. This is not a metaphor. It is technical reality. A model trained on data that excludes your language will make worse decisions about you. A healthcare system trained on data from wealthier populations will misdiagnose you more often. An algorithm trained to optimize for engagement on platforms you cannot access will still govern the information ecosystem you eventually enter. Exclusion at the design stage does not stay at the design stage. It travels forward.

What is required is not charity. It is not even altruism. It is the recognition that a technology being built on the premise of universal benefit should, at minimum, universally consult the people it intends to benefit. The researchers studying African languages are not asking for handouts. They are asking to be heard in the rooms where the decisions are being made. The engineers in Nairobi and Lagos and Dhaka are not asking to be saved. They are asking for the same access to computing power and investment that their counterparts in San Francisco take for granted.

The future of AI is not a destination that some countries will reach and others will miss. It is a set of choices being made right now, by specific people, in specific institutions, about who counts as a user and who counts as an afterthought. The nursing student in northern Nigeria typing in Hausa is not the edge case. She is the majority of the world. The question worth asking is not whether AI will eventually reach her. It is who gets to decide what it says when it does.

Glossary

Digital Divide ↑ Back to text

The gap between populations that have meaningful access to digital technology — reliable internet, capable devices, and the skills to use them — and those that do not. The digital divide is not binary but a spectrum: a person might have a smartphone but no broadband, or broadband but no electricity stability, or connectivity but no content in their language. In the context of AI, the divide extends to include access to AI tools, AI-generated services, and the economic benefits that AI-enabled industries produce.

Generative AI ↑ Back to text

A class of artificial intelligence systems that can generate new content — text, images, audio, video, and code — in response to prompts or instructions. Unlike earlier AI systems that classified or analyzed existing data, generative AI creates new outputs by learning patterns from vast training datasets. Examples include large language models (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini), image generators (Midjourney, DALL-E), and video generators. These systems are increasingly integrated into healthcare, education, legal, financial, and creative industries worldwide.

AI Readiness Index ↑ Back to text

A composite metric developed by the International Monetary Fund to measure a country's capacity to adopt, benefit from, and govern artificial intelligence. The index assesses four pillars: digital infrastructure (internet access, device penetration, data center capacity), human capital (digital literacy, STEM education, AI research output), regulatory environment (AI policy frameworks, data protection law, intellectual property systems), and economic integration (e-commerce adoption, digital financial services, technology export capability). Scores range from 0 to 1, with advanced economies averaging 0.68 and low-income countries averaging 0.32.

Large Language Model (LLM) ↑ Back to text

A type of generative AI system trained on massive quantities of text data to understand and produce human language. LLMs learn statistical patterns across billions of words — which words tend to follow which other words, in what contexts, and with what meanings — and use these patterns to generate coherent, contextually appropriate responses. Because they learn from existing text, their capabilities, biases, and blind spots are directly shaped by what text was available for training. An LLM trained primarily on English-language text will be dramatically more capable in English than in Hausa, Wolof, or any of the thousands of languages underrepresented in digital datasets.

Low-Resource Language ↑ Back to text

A term used in computational linguistics to describe languages for which there is insufficient digitized text, audio, or annotated data to train effective AI systems. The label "low-resource" describes a data condition, not a linguistic one — Hausa, for example, is spoken by over 70 million people, yet it is classified as low-resource because limited Hausa text has been digitized and made available in forms AI systems can learn from. The causes are structural: colonial language policies, underinvestment in African and Indigenous language digitization, and AI companies' prioritization of economically larger language markets.

Compute Gap ↑ Back to text

The disparity in access to computational hardware — servers, GPUs, data centers, and cloud computing resources — between high-income and low-income nations. Training modern AI systems requires enormous amounts of computing power: GPT-4, for example, was trained using tens of thousands of high-end GPUs over months. Countries without access to this hardware cannot train their own AI models, must rely on systems built elsewhere, or must pay for cloud compute at prices that may be prohibitive. The compute gap is a direct bottleneck to AI sovereignty — a country's ability to build AI systems that reflect its own values, languages, and priorities.
