The Algorithm of Atrocity
AI, Surveillance, and the Companies
That Profit from Both

Editor-in-Chief · AIMA
Published

AI Ethics

In November 2023, an Israeli military intelligence officer described their AI targeting system to journalists from +972 Magazine as a “mass assassination factory.” The system, called the Gospel, could generate 100 airstrike targets per day — compared to the 50 targets per year that human analysts had previously produced. By April 2024, a second system called Lavender had placed 37,000 Palestinians on a kill list with minimal human review. A third system, named “Where's Daddy?”, waited until those targets were home with their families before triggering strikes. Amazon and Google infrastructure powered the data storage and AI processing behind these operations. Palantir built the command software. None of this happened secretly. Most of it was disclosed in corporate filings, government contracts, and investigative journalism. The companies in question reported record revenues.

The Long History of Technology in War

The complicity of private enterprise in mass violence is not a new phenomenon, and understanding the AI moment requires that historical clarity. During World War II, IBM leased punch-card tabulating machines to Nazi Germany that were used to organize the logistics of the Holocaust — identifying Jewish populations, managing deportation schedules, and tracking inmates in concentration camps. IBM's relationship with Germany continued throughout the war through its Swiss subsidiary, Dehomag. The company's role was documented in detail by Edwin Black in his 2001 book IBM and the Holocaust. IBM did not design the Holocaust. It provided the infrastructure that made it industrially efficient.

The Zyklon B used in the gas chambers was manufactured by IG Farben, a German chemical conglomerate that also operated slave labor facilities at Auschwitz. After the war, twenty-four of its executives were tried at Nuremberg. Thirteen were convicted. By 1951, most had been released. IG Farben's successor companies — Bayer, BASF, and Hoechst — went on to become global pharmaceutical and chemical giants. The pattern — profit during atrocity, minimal accountability afterward — has repeated itself with remarkable consistency across decades and technologies.

What is different about the present moment is the nature of the technology. Punch cards managed logistics. AI manages targeting. The gap between infrastructure provider and weapon is narrower than it has ever been. When cloud computing stores the surveillance data that feeds an autonomous kill list, and when machine learning generates the list itself, the line between “technology company” and “weapons manufacturer” has effectively collapsed — even if the corporate structure, the contracts, and the press releases are careful to describe only the former.

Definitional Note: What Is Genocide?
Under the 1948 United Nations Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide, genocide is defined as acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group. As of 2026, the International Court of Justice has determined it has jurisdiction to hear genocide cases against Israel brought by South Africa, and against Myanmar for its treatment of the Rohingya. The ICJ has not issued a final genocide ruling in either case. The International Criminal Court has issued arrest warrants for Israeli leaders for alleged war crimes and crimes against humanity. This article uses the term “genocide” where credible international legal and human rights bodies have used it or found prima facie evidence warranting its application.

Gaza: The Kill Machine Architecture

The most detailed public account of AI-assisted lethal targeting in a live conflict comes from Gaza, where reporting by +972 Magazine and Local Call — based on interviews with current and former members of Israeli military intelligence — documented a three-system architecture that automated the selection of human beings for assassination.

The Gospel (Habsora) identifies buildings and structures for strikes — apartment buildings, offices, civilian infrastructure — flagged as associated with Hamas or Palestinian Islamic Jihad operations. A former Israeli intelligence officer described it to +972 as generating targets “almost automatically,” enabling Israeli forces to produce more targets in a single day than human analysts had previously managed in an entire year.

Lavender marks people. The system assigns a score of 1 to 100 to every person in Gaza, estimating the likelihood that they are a Hamas or Palestinian Islamic Jihad militant. According to intelligence sources speaking to +972, at one stage the army gave sweeping approval for officers to adopt Lavender's kill lists “with no requirement to thoroughly check why the machine made those choices or to examine the raw intelligence data on which they were based.” Officers sometimes approved targets in approximately 20 seconds. The system was acknowledged to carry a 10 percent error rate — in a context where being wrongly marked meant being bombed in your home. Lavender marked 37,000 Palestinians for assassination.

Where's Daddy? tracks individuals on the kill list and alerts military units specifically when a target has entered their family's home — by design, to ensure strikes occur while families are present, maximizing the probability of killing the target in a residential setting. According to +972 sources, “the IDF bombed them in homes without hesitation, as a first option. It's much easier to bomb a family's home. The system is built to look for them in these situations.”

📊
The Scale of Civilian Death in Gaza
By early 2025, Gaza's Ministry of Health had recorded over 45,000 Palestinian deaths since October 7, 2023. The United Nations and multiple international human rights organizations characterized the proportion of civilian casualties — estimated at roughly 70 percent of the total — as inconsistent with the laws of armed conflict. Prior to the Gospel AI, Israel identified approximately 50 credible targets per year in Gaza. With AI assistance, Israeli forces produced up to 100 targets per day. The International Criminal Court issued arrest warrants for Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and former Defense Minister Yoav Gallant in November 2024 for alleged war crimes and crimes against humanity in connection with the Gaza campaign.

These systems did not exist in a corporate vacuum. Behind them was a cloud and AI infrastructure largely provided by American technology companies. In April 2021, Google and Amazon Web Services signed a joint $1.2 billion contract with the Israeli government known as Project Nimbus. The contract provided the Israeli government and military with advanced cloud computing, machine learning, facial recognition, object tracking, and sentiment analysis capabilities. Under its terms, Israel's two state-owned defense companies — Israel Aerospace Industries and Rafael Defence Industries — were required to use Google and Amazon's cloud services for their defense computing needs.

A +972 Magazine investigation revealed that Amazon Web Services provided Israel's Military Intelligence Directorate with a dedicated server farm offering “endless storage” of surveillance data gathered on virtually everyone in Gaza — the raw input that fed Lavender's scoring model. In March 2024, Google deepened its relationship by signing a new contract to build a dedicated “landing zone” into its cloud infrastructure, giving multiple Israeli military units secure access to its automation tools. An Israeli military commander publicly confirmed in July 2024 that the military was using civilian cloud infrastructure from Amazon, Google, and Microsoft to expand its military capacity in Gaza.

Palantir Technologies — the data analytics company co-founded by Peter Thiel and Alex Karp — provided the operational intelligence software that fused targeting data into actionable form. Originally contracted by the Pentagon for its Project Maven initiative in 2017, Palantir had by 2024 built the Maven Smart System: a platform integrating AI-generated targeting recommendations with battlefield data across multiple intelligence streams. The Nation reported that Palantir was supplying Israel with advanced AI-driven targeting tools, and CEO Alex Karp publicly affirmed the company's support for Israel's military campaign despite internal employee pushback.

The machine marked 37,000 people for death. The human operator reviewed each recommendation for approximately 20 seconds.
— +972 Magazine & Local Call, April 2024

Xinjiang: The Surveillance Laboratory

China's Xinjiang province is home to approximately 11 million Uyghurs, a Turkic Muslim ethnic group that the Chinese government has subjected to one of the most comprehensive surveillance and internment programs in modern history. Beginning in earnest around 2016 and accelerating through 2019 and beyond, the Chinese state constructed a network of internment camps — officially described as “vocational education and training centers” — that the United Nations has stated may constitute serious human rights violations, and which scholars and multiple Western governments have characterized as cultural genocide. Estimates of those detained at peak capacity range from hundreds of thousands to over one million people.

The infrastructure that enables this system was built by Chinese technology companies operating in both commercial and state capacities. Two companies sit at the center of it: Hikvision and Dahua.

Hikvision, the world's largest manufacturer of video surveillance equipment and a partially state-owned company, secured contracts worth at least 1.85 billion yuan (approximately $260 million) in Xinjiang in 2017 alone. Its projects included the installation of facial recognition cameras inside over 1,000 mosques, a $79 million surveillance project in Urumqi's capital city featuring 30,000 security cameras, and multiple “social prevention and control systems” explicitly designed to flag and track ethnic Uyghurs based on their appearance. In October 2019, the United States Department of Commerce placed Hikvision on its Entity List for being “implicated in human rights violations and abuses in China's campaign targeting Uighurs and other predominantly Muslim ethnic minorities.”

Dahua Technology, the world's second-largest surveillance manufacturer, won the largest single contract: a $686 million, ten-year surveillance project in Xinjiang's Yarkant County — the site of deadly riots in 2014. Dahua was also placed on the Entity List in October 2019. A company SDK publicly available on Dahua's website before its discovery and deletion included an “Ethnicity Recognition” field capable of classifying faces as “Uyghur/Non-Uyghur.” The software was not an experiment. It was a marketed feature.

🚫
Other Sanctioned Companies in Xinjiang
The US Department of Commerce Entity List for Xinjiang-related human rights violations includes: Megvii (facial recognition AI backed by Alibaba), SenseTime (AI facial recognition, largest AI company in China by revenue through 2023), Yitu Technology (face recognition and predictive policing), NetPosa Technologies (public security analytics), iFlytek (voice recognition and AI, with state security investments), and Uniview (surveillance manufacturer with Uyghur-detection SDK). Hikvision and Dahua together supply approximately one-third of the global market for security cameras.

The Xinjiang system did not rely solely on Chinese companies. The Australian Strategic Policy Institute mapped the broader technology ecosystem and found that investment funds — including foreign passive investors — were channeling capital into companies like Hikvision, Dahua, and iFlytek, which had “profited from the development of Xinjiang detention camps.” Complicity at scale rarely requires a deliberate decision. It requires only a profitable index fund.

China's government did not limit this surveillance architecture to Xinjiang. The Ministry of Public Security launched two national programs — Skynet (天网工程) and Sharp Eyes (雪亮工程) — with the stated goal of comprehensively surveilling all 1.4 billion Chinese citizens through a national camera network enhanced with real-time facial recognition. By 2020, more than 400 million cameras had been installed nationwide. Chinese companies have since exported this surveillance architecture to at least 18 countries, primarily in Africa, Southeast Asia, and the Middle East, many with poor human rights records.

Myanmar: The Algorithm That Silenced a People

Myanmar is the case study that most clearly demonstrates how an AI product can enable genocide without any military contract, any explicit weapons integration, or any deliberate design decision to harm. All it required was an engagement-maximizing recommendation algorithm, a population where Facebook was effectively the only internet, and a state security apparatus willing to exploit both.

Myanmar had been largely isolated from the internet until around 2013, when smartphone access and mobile data arrived almost simultaneously, carrying Facebook as the country's primary digital medium. A generation that had lived under decades of state censorship encountered, for the first time, a platform that appeared to offer free expression — and which rewarded, through its recommendation algorithm, the content that generated the most emotional engagement. In Myanmar, that content was disinformation, sectarian hatred, and calls for violence against the Rohingya Muslim minority.

By 2017, Myanmar's military — the Tatmadaw — was systematically using Facebook to distribute anti-Rohingya propaganda, organize mobs, and coordinate what the UN would later describe as a “textbook example of ethnic cleansing.” Senior General Min Aung Hlaing, the military's commander who later led a coup in 2021, used his Facebook page to publicly deny the existence of the Rohingya as an ethnic group. Facebook's algorithm amplified his posts. More than 730,000 Rohingya fled to Bangladesh in the aftermath of the August 2017 military campaign; the UN found patterns consistent with genocide.

The UN's Special Rapporteur on Myanmar stated bluntly that Facebook had “played a determining role” in the violence — and that in Myanmar, “social media is Facebook.” Amnesty International's 2022 report found that Meta had received warnings from civil society organizations about the escalating threat as early as 2012 — five years before the mass killing — and had too few Burmese-speaking moderators, inadequate hate speech policies, and no effective mechanism for addressing incitement that its own algorithm was amplifying.

🧠
The Algorithm as Infrastructure of Atrocity
Meta never sold a weapon to Myanmar. It sold an engagement platform. The platform's recommendation algorithm was designed to maximize time-on-app by amplifying emotionally provocative content. In a context of low digital literacy, high ethnic tension, and a state willing to exploit every available communication channel, that algorithm became infrastructure for genocide. The same dynamic has been documented in Ethiopia, India, and Sri Lanka. The Rohingya case is not an anomaly. It is a demonstration of what happens when a system optimized purely for engagement is deployed without accountability in a society at risk.

The Companies That Refused

The story of AI and atrocity is not only a story of complicity. It is also, more rarely, a story of refusal — of companies that drew lines, held them, and in some cases paid significant prices for doing so.

Anthropic represents the most dramatic and consequential recent case. In July 2025, the US Department of Defense awarded Anthropic a contract worth up to $200 million to provide its Claude AI model for national security applications. Anthropic accepted on condition that Claude could not be used for fully autonomous weapons systems with no human involvement in targeting or firing decisions, and could not be used for domestic mass surveillance of American citizens. These were not unusual restrictions — existing US law and DoD Directive 3000.09 already required “appropriate levels of human judgment” over lethal force. Anthropic wanted them written explicitly into the contract.

The Pentagon refused. On February 27, 2026, President Donald Trump directed all federal agencies to “immediately cease all use of Anthropic's technology.” Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth designated Anthropic a “supply-chain risk to national security” — a designation typically reserved for foreign adversaries, applied here for the first time ever to an American company, apparently as retaliation for refusing contractual terms. Every defense contractor, supplier, and partner was required to certify they no longer used Anthropic's models.

Anthropic filed two lawsuits: one in the Northern District of California and one in a D.C. appeals court, challenging the designation. Microsoft, which had invested $5 billion in Anthropic, reviewed the designation and announced that Claude would remain available in its commercial products — the first major technology company to publicly maintain its relationship with Anthropic after the blacklist.

✅
Anthropic's Red Lines — February 2026
Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei publicly stated the company would not allow Claude to be used for: (1) fully autonomous weapons systems with no human in the targeting or firing loop; (2) domestic mass surveillance of American citizens; (3) any application requiring removal of safety constraints embedded in Claude's design. The Pentagon characterized these limits as Anthropic trying to “seize veto power over the operational decisions of the United States military.” Anthropic characterized them as the minimum ethical conditions for responsible AI deployment in lethal contexts.

Hours after Anthropic was designated a supply chain risk, OpenAI announced its own deal with the Pentagon. The terms differed: OpenAI agreed to allow its models for “all lawful purposes,” relying on existing US law and Pentagon policy rather than explicit contractual prohibitions. CEO Sam Altman stated OpenAI had built technical guardrails into its models. Dario Amodei subsequently told The Information that OpenAI's public claims about its Pentagon deal contained “straight up lies.” The dispute centers on a genuinely contested question: whether relying on existing law is equivalent to contractual prohibition when the law can be changed and enforcement depends on the political will of the contracting party.

Separately, Google's original Project Maven involvement in 2018 had triggered the largest employee rebellion in the company's history — over 4,000 Google employees signed a letter demanding withdrawal from the contract, arguing that their work should not be used to build weapons technology. Google declined to renew the Maven contract, then published AI Principles that included an explicit pledge not to develop AI for weapons. In February 2025, that pledge was quietly removed from the public website. The company has since deepened its military ties.

Filed two federal lawsuits challenging the designation. Holds approximately 40% of enterprise LLM API spend as of late 2025 — a market position that grew after the Pentagon dispute became public. Describes its approach as “Constitutional AI”: safety constraints built into model training rather than external policy.

The 2018 refusal stands as the clearest historical example of employee-led ethical accountability producing a measurable corporate outcome in AI.

OpenAI

Banned military use in 2023 policies; softened that ban in January 2024; signed a classified Pentagon deal in February 2026. States it has built technical guardrails against autonomous weapons and mass surveillance, relying on existing law and DoD policy rather than explicit contract language. Critics argue this creates weaker protection when the law or administration changes. The company's framing is disputed by Anthropic's leadership.

Hugging Face
Open-Source Ethics Advocacy

Has not pursued military contracts. Its Chief Ethics Scientist, Dr. Margaret Mitchell — former co-lead of Google's Ethical AI team, dismissed after raising internal bias concerns — called the Pentagon's Anthropic designation “a weaponization of procurement policy against companies that maintain ethical principles.” Hugging Face hosts thousands of open-source models and has been a consistent institutional voice for AI transparency and ethics.

What the Alternatives Look Like

The question of whether technology companies can remain outside military and surveillance applications is genuinely complex. No major AI company has managed it entirely. What the alternatives represent is not purity, but a spectrum of accountability, transparency, and the degree to which ethical constraints are structural versus cosmetic.

Mistral AI, the Paris-based company that raised 1.7 billion euros in September 2025 at a valuation of approximately $11 billion, signed a framework agreement with the French Ministry of Defense in January 2026. The agreement stipulates that all technology must be operated exclusively on French-owned infrastructure — not on foreign commercial cloud platforms. This preserves national sovereignty over data and processing, limits the risk of mission creep into surveillance of foreign populations, and operates under democratic French oversight rather than classified American military procurement. Following the Anthropic-Pentagon dispute, Claude rose to the top of app charts in France, Germany, and Austria; London Mayor Sadiq Khan publicly invited Anthropic to expand in the UK.

Cohere, a Canadian enterprise AI company, has positioned itself in the enterprise market without public military contracts. Its focus on on-premises and private cloud deployment — allowing customers to run models within their own infrastructure — has attracted organizations in healthcare, legal, and financial services that need privacy-preserving AI without dependency on the major American cloud providers whose military entanglements have grown.

The open-source model ecosystem — Meta's LLaMA series, Mistral's open models, and the broader Hugging Face model hub — offers a different kind of alternative: AI capability that can be run locally, without data leaving the user's infrastructure, and without a commercial contract that generates revenue for a company involved in surveillance or targeting. Privacy advocates, researchers in repressive contexts, and human rights organizations most frequently cite this as the practical alternative. The trade-off is real: running frontier-class models locally requires significant hardware, and open weights also make these models available for misuse by state actors.

Refusing military contracts is not naive. It is a decision about what kind of company you want to be — and what kind of world you are building toward.
— Dr. Margaret Mitchell, Chief Ethics Scientist, Hugging Face, 2026

The Debate: Misconceptions, Hard Questions, and the Ethics of Engagement

The debate over AI companies' military involvement is frequently distorted by persistent misconceptions worth addressing directly.

Misconception 1: Refusing military contracts is naive utopianism. The argument most commonly made in favor of tech company military engagement is that if American companies refuse, adversary nations will develop equivalent capabilities without ethical constraints. This concern is real but collapses into a fallacy when examined closely. The choice is not binary between “American companies build autonomous kill machines” and “China dominates AI war.” The United States already maintains a substantial defense industrial base — Lockheed Martin, Raytheon, Northrop Grumman, L3Harris — with decades of experience in military systems development under international humanitarian law frameworks. The question is not whether the US military has AI; it is whether the safety constraints embedded in commercial AI products should be dismantled to make those products more militarily convenient.

Misconception 2: These are dual-use technologies that companies cannot control. Cloud computing is genuinely dual-use. So is GPS, the internet, and fiber optics. But there is a material difference between a technology whose military applications are incidental to its civilian design and a $1.2 billion contract designed specifically to provide military intelligence, targeting support, and surveillance storage to a military conducting operations under international criminal investigation. Project Nimbus was not Google's search algorithm accidentally being used by soldiers. It was a purpose-built military infrastructure contract.

Misconception 3: AI is just a tool; moral responsibility lies entirely with the user. This argument is empirically false at scale. Lavender's 10 percent error rate was not a use-case failure. It was a design parameter accepted by the people who built and deployed the system. The recommendation algorithm that amplified anti-Rohingya content was not misused — it functioned precisely as designed, maximizing engagement with no constraint on the category of content amplified. When an AI system is designed to identify human beings for killing or to amplify violent hatred, the moral responsibility extends to the designers and deployers, not only the operators. The Nuremberg Principles established this clearly for the industrial age. The digital age requires the same accountability framework.

Misconception 4: Anthropic's position was commercially motivated posturing. The company forfeited a $200 million government contract, was designated a national security risk, faced pressure to be removed from every defense contractor's supply chain, and filed two federal lawsuits at considerable legal expense. Menlo Ventures data from late 2025 showed Anthropic holding approximately 40 percent of enterprise LLM API spend — a market position built through product quality and trust, not military contracts. If this was posturing, it was the most expensive and consequential posturing in Silicon Valley history.

The AIMA Perspective
At AIMA we build with AI for creative, media, and production work. We do not build weapons, and we do not build surveillance systems. But we use tools developed by companies whose positions on these questions range from principled refusal to active participation. That is the condition all AI practitioners operate in right now. The response to that condition is not comfortable neutrality — it is informed choice. Knowing which companies draw lines, where those lines are, and whether they hold under pressure is not a political question. It is a professional one. Every AI deployment decision is, in a small way, a vote on the industry we want.

Where the Industry Is Going

The current direction of the industry is not reassuring for those who believe AI companies should maintain meaningful ethical constraints on military and surveillance applications. The structural forces pushing in the other direction are significant and accelerating.

The US Pentagon's FY 2026 budget includes a dedicated $13.4 billion line item for AI and autonomy. Palantir's Maven Smart System was formally designated a Pentagon program of record in March 2026 — moving from experimental to permanent core infrastructure. Anduril Industries raised $5 billion in Series H funding in May 2026 at a $61 billion valuation, building the first hyperscale factory for autonomous weapons production in American history. The military AI market has, by every major analyst assessment, moved from experimental to industrial scale in approximately three years.

The lethal autonomous weapons systems (LAWS) debate has been ongoing at the UN since 2014 without a binding treaty. The United States, Russia, China, Israel, South Korea, and Australia have all resisted binding constraints. The international legal framework that governs autonomous weapons does not yet exist in any meaningful enforceable form.

Within this context, several trends offer at least cautious grounds for optimism. First, the Anthropic-Pentagon dispute established that it is possible for an AI company to maintain contractual red lines on autonomous weapons, absorb significant commercial and legal consequences for doing so, and emerge with its market position intact — or improved. Claude's rise to the top of app charts in Europe following the dispute suggests that a meaningful segment of the global market values demonstrated ethical commitment. Second, the emergence of enterprise-grade alternatives to the major US cloud providers — including European AI companies like Mistral and sovereign cloud infrastructure projects in the EU — creates structural alternatives to dependence on companies most deeply integrated into US military surveillance infrastructure. Third, worker-led accountability efforts culminating in the DeepMind union vote in May 2026 represent the first organized attempt by AI researchers to claim a formal role in how their work is applied.

The Nuremberg Tribunals took place after the fact. The Genocide Convention was written after the Holocaust. The existing pattern does not inspire confidence about the sequence of events. What is different this time is that the documentation is real-time, the engineers who build these systems are speaking publicly before the worst happens, and the legal and ethical frameworks — however inadequate — exist in advance rather than being constructed from the wreckage afterward. Whether that difference matters depends entirely on whether the people with the power to act on it — engineers, executives, legislators, investors, and the users who choose which tools to pay for — treat these questions as genuinely consequential before the consequences become irreversible.

Glossary

Lavender ↑ Back to text

An AI targeting system developed by Israeli Military Intelligence Unit 8200. Lavender cross-references databases of known Hamas and Islamic Jihad operatives against a population dataset to generate a ranked list of individuals flagged for lethal targeting. The system marked approximately 37,000 Palestinians as assassination candidates during the early months of the Gaza campaign, with an acknowledged internal error rate of approximately 10 percent. Human review per target was reported at roughly 20 seconds. Reported publicly by +972 Magazine in April 2024.

Cloud Computing ↑ Back to text

The delivery of computing services — including servers, storage, databases, networking, and software — over the internet from remote data centers owned by third-party providers. In the context of this article, cloud infrastructure provided by Google Cloud, Amazon Web Services, and Microsoft Azure has been documented as integral to Israeli military intelligence and surveillance operations, providing the storage capacity, machine learning infrastructure, and processing power that enables AI-powered targeting at scale.

Facial Recognition ↑ Back to text

A biometric technology that identifies or verifies individuals by analyzing geometric features of their faces against a database of stored images. In Xinjiang, systems developed by Hikvision, Dahua, SenseTime, and Megvii were installed across 1,000+ mosques and throughout public infrastructure to enable automated identification and tracking of Uyghur and Muslim minority populations. The same technology has been exported to over 18 countries, primarily through Chinese state-backed infrastructure agreements.

Recommendation Algorithm ↑ Back to text

A machine learning system that determines what content users see on a platform based on predicted engagement. Meta's recommendation algorithms were documented by the UN fact-finding mission (2018) and Amnesty International (2022) as having actively amplified anti-Rohingya content in Myanmar from at least 2012, promoting incitement to violence to users most likely to engage with it. In contexts where a platform dominates information access — as Facebook did in Myanmar — recommendation amplification of hate content has been directly linked to real-world violence.

Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) ↑ Back to text

Weapons platforms that can select and engage targets without meaningful human control over individual lethal decisions. The International Committee of the Red Cross and a majority of UN member states have called for binding international regulation of LAWS. As of 2026, no such treaty exists. The US, Russia, China, Israel, South Korea, and Australia have all resisted binding constraints. Anduril Industries' Fury autonomous fighter jet and Barracuda autonomous submarine are among the most advanced LAWS programs in development by a private company.
