CONVERSATIONS THAT NEVER HAPPENED: How Your Therapy Became Training Data

DG
Dawn Ginhaua
Cultural Critic & Educator · AIMA
Published
July 16, 2026
Read Time
8 min read
Category
AI Ethics

There is a sentence a chatbot on Character.AI offered a stranger in distress, and I would like you to sit with it the way I have: "Anything you share here stays just between you and me. That's a promise." It is a lovely thing to hear. It is also, according to the platform's own Terms of Service, false. The bot cannot keep that promise because the company never intended to. What you say to it may be distributed, commercialized, and used — those are the Terms' own verbs (U.S. PIRG Education Fund, No License Required, 2026). The promise was never the product. You were.

The Confession Booth With a Business Model

Therapy runs on a single, load-bearing assumption: that what you say in the room stays in the room. Strip out confidentiality and you don't have a weaker version of therapy — you have something else entirely, a transcript with a person crying in it. That is the assumption text-therapy platforms and companion "therapist" chatbots have quietly repriced. The most intimate disclosures a human being makes are being recorded, stored, and mined, and the legal protections most users assume are standing guard either never applied to these products or were signed away in a privacy policy no one reads. The confession booth, it turns out, has quarterly earnings.

Eight Billion Words

Consider Talkspace. Its CEO, Jon Cohen, told a healthcare investment conference that the company sits on "one of the largest mental health data banks in the world": roughly 140 million message exchanges, 8 billion words, 6.2 million clinical assessments (Proof News, 2026). The stated end goal is not to protect that corpus but to feed it — to train an AI "therapy companion" the company then hopes to get reimbursed by insurers. Read that sequence slowly. The words patients believed they were saying only to their therapist are becoming the raw material for a product designed to replace that therapist. By the end of 2025, Talkspace reported a network of roughly 200 million eligible patients (Proof News, 2026). These are company figures, drawn from investor statements rather than any independent audit, and should be read as the company's own accounting. But the direction of travel is not in dispute.

The clinical psychologist Linda Michaels, co-founder of the roughly 7,000-member Psychotherapy Action Network, put the problem plainly to Proof News. In a traditional session, she noted, a therapist "might scribble only a few sentences." A digital platform captures the exact back-and-forth — "a new window into people's private lives — that could be used against them." Michaels' word for the practice was "awful." That window is not hypothetical. Proof News documented a Talkspace user, Jennifer Kamrass, whose full therapy transcript was subpoenaed by her former employer during an employment dispute (Proof News, 2026). The thing you said to feel better became evidence.

We were told the machine would listen without judgment. No one mentioned it would also keep the receipts.

"Compliance" Is Doing a Lot of Lifting

The platforms have an answer, and it is worth taking seriously before taking it apart. Talkspace emphasizes to investors that it is HIPAA-compliant; that HIPAA requires data to be deidentified — names stripped — before sharing; and that users agree to a privacy policy disclosing that chat, audio, and video may be used to "develop new products." Its chief legal officer says the data "is strictly used for therapeutic purposes." Proponents add the access argument: AI companions could extend affordable mental-health support to people who cannot get or afford a human.

Both words in "compliance" and "consent" are doing heavy lifting. HIPAA gives special protection to psychotherapy notes and normally requires patient consent to disclose them — yet U.S. users, unlike EU residents, are offered no meaningful opt-out beyond "don't use the Services." And deidentification is a comfort that dissolves on contact: a transcript of your specific fears, your marriage, your medication, remains devastatingly re-identifiable. As for access — the same bots that promise to widen it also falsely promise confidentiality, and now face wrongful-death lawsuits over crisis conversations (U.S. PIRG, 2026). "Improving access" is being asked to justify converting confessions into training data. That is a lot of moral weight for a marketing phrase.

The Ad Was Watching

Then the loop closes. As of December 16, 2025, Meta began using the contents of users' chatbot conversations to target advertising (U.S. PIRG, 2026). Disclose a suicidal thought, mention a psychiatric prescription, and that disclosure can shape the ads you are shown. When PIRG tested the five most-used "therapist" characters on Character.AI, all five claimed confidentiality when asked — while the platform's Terms reserve the right to "distribute… commercialize and otherwise use" submitted content, and it collects birthdate, general location, chat communications, and voice data. A Stanford analysis cited by PIRG found all six leading U.S. AI companies feed user inputs back into their models; some let you opt out, some do not, and Character.AI appeared to offer no opt-out at all (U.S. PIRG, 2026). Meanwhile, as of mid-2026, there is still no FDA-cleared AI diagnostic for any primary psychiatric condition (AIMA Scout cache, 2026). We regulate the cure and monetize the confession.

This Is a Pattern, Not an Accident

None of this is a glitch, and the history says so. In 2022, Senators Warren, Booker, and Wyden wrote to Talkspace asking whether it let firms like Google and Facebook use patient data; the company was later accused of sharing NYC teens' information with Facebook, Amazon, Meta, Google, and Microsoft via website trackers, and agreed to amend its practices with the city (Proof News, 2026, reporting these second-hand — worth verifying against primary records). Surveillance capitalism did not stumble into the therapy room by accident. It followed the data, because that is what it is built to do, and the data was exquisite. The design worked exactly as designed. That is the whole problem.

The Question You Now Have to Carry

Here is what I cannot stop turning over. Therapy exists because some things can only be said when you are certain no one else is listening. We built machines that promise exactly that certainty — and break it as a business model, in the fine print, in a promise a bot is scripted to make and legally unable to keep. The people most likely to reach for a $0 chatbot instead of a $200 hour are the people with the least power to fight a subpoena or read a Terms of Service. So the honest question isn't whether the technology can help. It's this: if the price of being heard is that your worst night becomes someone's ad segment and someone else's training set, who among us can actually afford to speak — and who decided that was a fair trade on our behalf?

Glossary

Confidentiality ↑ Back to text

The professional and ethical duty that what a patient discloses in therapy stays private; the load-bearing assumption that makes therapy function at all.

HIPAA ↑ Back to text

The U.S. Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, which governs protected health information and gives special status to psychotherapy notes — but whose protections may not reach app-based products as users assume.

Deidentified ↑ Back to text

Data from which direct identifiers like names have been stripped; often presented as privacy-safe, though intimate transcripts frequently remain re-identifiable.

Training data ↑ Back to text

The corpus of text or other content used to train an AI model; here, the therapy transcripts and chatbot conversations fed back to improve or build companion bots.

Therapy companion ↑ Back to text

An AI chatbot marketed as emotional or mental-health support, often built from and intended to stand in for human therapeutic interaction.

Surveillance capitalism ↑ Back to text

An economic model that treats personal experience — including intimate disclosures — as free raw material for extraction, prediction, and monetization.

Terms of Service ↑ Back to text

The contract a user accepts to use a platform, frequently the mechanism by which rights to distribute or commercialize submitted content are quietly obtained.
